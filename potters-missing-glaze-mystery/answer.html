Mission 01 · Easy · Art & Culture / Internet Research

The Plate on the Shelf

Answer: Delft Blue
How to get there

Search something like "blue and white Dutch pottery." The top results describe Delftware / Delft Blue: white tin-glazed earthenware painted with cobalt-blue windmills, ships, and floral patterns, made in and around the city of Delft since the 1600s, inspired by imported Chinese porcelain.

Why this is the only answer

The description (white background, single blue color, windmills/ships/flowers) is a textbook match for Delft Blue and rules out other pottery styles, which use different colors or motifs. Accept "Delft Blue" (also fine: "Delftware").

Source / fact used

Verified via web search across multiple sources, e.g. iamExpat: "A brief history of Delft Blue" and Dutch Delftware: "What is Delftware?" — both confirm the blue-and-white, tin-glazed, Delft-city origin of the style.

Mission 02 · Easy · Logical Reasoning (Foundational — classification)

Five Jars, One Odd Jar Out

Answer: Sponge
How to get there

Silica, feldspar, whiting, and iron oxide are all real ceramic glaze materials (silica is the glass-former, feldspar the flux, whiting the calcium source, iron oxide a colorant). A sponge is a studio tool used for cleanup and smoothing, never mixed into a glaze recipe.

Why this is the only answer

It's the only item of the five that isn't a powder/material you'd weigh into a glaze batch — pure classification by function, solvable without any lookup.

Source / fact used

General ceramics knowledge, cross-checked via web search, e.g. The Ceramic School: "Understanding Glaze Composition", confirming silica, feldspar, whiting, and iron oxide as standard glaze ingredients.

Mission 03 · Medium · Dutch Language

The Smudged Word

Answer: het glazuur
How to get there

"Glazuur" (glaze/enamel) is a neuter Dutch noun, so it takes "het," not "de." A search for "de of het glazuur" or a Dutch dictionary (Van Dale, Woordenlijst.nl) confirms this directly.

Why this is the only answer

De/het is not a matter of opinion — it's a fixed grammatical property of the noun, and every reliable Dutch dictionary agrees "glazuur" is a het-word. The mission asks the child to type the full two-word phrase to avoid ambiguity about which article was intended.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search, e.g. Ensie: "De of het glazuur?" and Woorden.org, both confirming "het glazuur" and noting the word is listed in the official Woordenlijst Nederlandse Taal as neuter.

Mission 04 · Medium · Mathematics (ratio/percentage, pen & paper)

Six Parts, Three Parts, One Part

Answer: 600 (grams)
How to get there

The ratio 6:3:1 has 10 total parts. Silica is 3 parts and weighs 300 g, so 1 part = 300 ÷ 3 = 100 g. Feldspar is 6 parts, so feldspar = 6 × 100 g = 600 g.

Why this is the only answer

Fixed ratio and one known weight force a single value for "1 part," which forces every other ingredient's weight — recomputed and confirmed: 100 g (whiting, 1 part) + 300 g (silica) + 600 g (feldspar) = 1,000 g total, consistent with the 10-part ratio.

Source / fact used

Self-contained arithmetic puzzle (fictional in-story recipe, not a real historical formula) — recomputed by hand to confirm.

Mission 05 · Medium · History / Internet Research

The World's Oldest Pot

Answer: China
How to get there

Search "oldest pottery in the world discovered." Reputable science sources report pottery fragments found in Xianrendong Cave, Jiangxi Province, China, radiocarbon-dated to roughly 19,000-20,000 years ago — the oldest pottery known.

Why this is the only answer

Multiple independent, reputable sources (a peer-reviewed Science journal article, the American Ceramic Society) agree on China/Xianrendong Cave as the current record-holder, so the search lands cleanly on one answer rather than a debate.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search: Science: "Early Pottery at 20,000 Years Ago in Xianrendong Cave, China" and American Ceramic Society: "Oldest known pottery dates back 20,000 years".

Mission 07 · Hard · Mathematics (geometry/measurement, diagram)

How Much Glaze for the Big Vase?

Answer: 517 (milliliters)
How to get there

Diameter = 14 cm, so radius = 7 cm. Using π ≈ 22/7: circumference = 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44 cm. Curved side area = circumference × height = 44 × 20 = 880 cm². Bottom circle area = π × r² = 22/7 × 7 × 7 = 154 cm². Total glazed area = 880 + 154 = 1,034 cm². At 2 cm² per mL: 1,034 ÷ 2 = 517 mL.

Why this is the only answer

Recomputed independently from the same stated numbers (diameter 14, height 20, coverage 2 cm²/mL) and confirmed 517 mL exactly, with every diagram dimension (radius 7 used in the SVG ellipse/line coordinates) consistent with the labeled 14 cm / 20 cm.

Source / fact used

Self-drawn inline-SVG diagram, all dimensions internally consistent; standard cylinder surface-area formulas (2πrh for the side, πr² for the base), recomputed by hand.

Mission 09 · Big "Aha!" · Logical Reasoning (Advanced — multi-constraint) + Cryptography (maker's-mark substitution) + Delayed Recall

The Kiln Schedule

Answer: Tuesday
How to get there

Decode key: △=4, ◆=6, ✦=8, ●=10. Clue "Mira's usual cone is used on Wednesday" requires recalling the Studio Notebook aside after Mission 2 ("Mira always fires her glaze tests to Cone 6") — so Wednesday = ◆ = Cone 6.

The Mug-two-days-before-Vase clue only fits (Mon,Wed) or (Tue,Thu). If Mug=Tue/Vase=Thu, then Bowl and Platter would have to fill Mon and Wed — but Platter can't be Wed (Wed's cone is 6, Platter's is ● =10) and can't be Mon either (explicit clue), which is a contradiction, so that pairing is impossible. Therefore Mug=Monday, Vase=Wednesday (=Cone 6). Bowl and Platter fill Tuesday/Thursday: Platter can't be Wed (already placed) — testing Platter=Tuesday would force Platter=✦=Cone 8 (the "day after Mug" clue), contradicting Platter=●=Cone 10, so Platter=Thursday and Bowl=Tuesday. The ✦ (Cone 8) clue then confirms Tuesday's piece (Bowl) is Cone 8, and Mug takes the last remaining cone, △=Cone 4.

Why this is the only answer

Solved by hand from only the five clues plus the decode key; every branch not chosen led to a direct contradiction with another clue (no guessing needed), confirming one forced schedule: Mug=Mon/Cone4, Bowl=Tue/Cone8, Vase=Wed/Cone6, Platter=Thu/Cone10.

The maker's-mark symbols (△ ◆ ✦ ●) are an invented pictograph substitution code in the spirit of real ancient pottery maker's-marks, not a real historical cipher.

Grand Finale · Multi-stage (2 stages) · Mathematics

The Last Torn Corner

Stage 1 answer: 2192 (°F)  |  Stage 2 (final) answer: 10 (hours)
How to get there

Stage 1: °F = (°C × 9 ÷ 5) + 32 = (1,200 × 9 ÷ 5) + 32 = (10,800 ÷ 5) + 32 = 2,160 + 32 = 2,192°F.

Stage 2: Timer rule is 1 hour per 200°F, rounded down. 2,192 ÷ 200 = 10.96, which rounds down to 10 hours. Stage 2 genuinely needs Stage 1's exact number (2,192) — a wrong Stage 1 answer produces a different division result and likely a different rounded-down hour count.

Why this is the only answer

Both stages are pure, recomputed arithmetic from fixed given numbers (1,200°C and the 200°F-per-hour rule) — no ambiguity, and the "round down" instruction removes any question about 10 vs. 11 hours.

Source / fact used

Standard Celsius-to-Fahrenheit conversion formula; self-contained in-story timer rule. Recomputed independently: 1,200 → 2,192°F → 10 hours, confirmed.
