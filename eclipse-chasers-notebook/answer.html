Mission 01 · Easy

The Shadow's First Footstep

Answer: Oregon
How to get there

Search "path of totality 2017 map" or "2017 total solar eclipse path". The path of totality for the August 21, 2017 eclipse crossed the U.S. mainland west to east, and the Moon's shadow first reached land on the Oregon coast (around 10:15 a.m. PDT, near Lincoln City/Newport).

Why this is the only answer

Oregon is the only U.S. state where the 2017 path of totality met the coastline at its western starting point — every other state on the path is further east, along the shadow's route, not its entry point.

Source / fact used

NASA/eclipse tracking sites (e.g. eclipsewise.com, greatamericaneclipse.com) confirm the 2017 path of totality began on the Oregon coast. Verified via web search of multiple independent eclipse-tracking sources.

Mission 07 · Hard

The Coded Postscript

Answer: sobral
How to get there

The message "SOBRAL" was encoded with a Caesar cipher shifted forward 5 letters (S→X, O→T, B→G, R→W, A→F, L→Q), giving "XTGWFQ". To decode, shift each letter of "XTGWFQ" backward by 5: X→S, T→O, G→B, W→R, F→A, Q→L, spelling "SOBRAL".

Why this is the only answer

A Caesar shift is a fixed, reversible substitution — with the shift amount given (5), there is exactly one plaintext that decodes to "XTGWFQ".

Source / fact used

Caesar cipher arithmetic, double-checked programmatically (encode "SOBRAL" +5 → "XTGWFQ"; decode "XTGWFQ" −5 → "SOBRAL"). "Sobral" is the real Brazilian town where part of the 1919 eclipse expedition observed (see Mission 8's source) — used here as the coded word.

Delayed-recall plant

The shift number 5 is explicitly flagged in-story ("I always use 5 for these — remember that number, Agent") for reuse, unprompted, two missions later in the Big "Aha!" Moment (Mission 9, Stage 2). That later mission does not restate the number or point back to this mission by name.

Mission 08 · Hard

Two Claims About 1919

Answer: Einstein
How to get there

Search "1919 solar eclipse Einstein" or "Eddington eclipse expedition". On May 29, 1919, two British teams — one led by Arthur Eddington on the island of Príncipe, the other by Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson in Sobral, Brazil — measured how much starlight bent as it passed close to the Sun during totality. Their results matched the amount predicted by Albert Einstein's theory of General Relativity, confirming it. Claim 1 is true; Claim 2 is false (the expedition was about starlight bending, not the Sun's size or distance).

Why this is the only answer

Every reputable source on the 1919 expedition (Royal Society, Britannica, Smithsonian) describes it as confirming light-bending / General Relativity, never as a measurement of the Sun's size or distance — so Claim 2 has no factual basis and Claim 1 is the unambiguous answer, with Einstein as the scientist.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search across multiple sources: Smithsonian Magazine ("A Total Solar Eclipse 100 Years Ago Proved Einstein's General Relativity"), Royal Society ("The 1919 eclipse results that verified general relativity"), and Britannica. Results were announced 6 November 1919 at a joint Royal Society/Royal Astronomical Society meeting.

The Trunk's Final Lock

Answer: 986
How to get there

The three biggest different digits from 1-9 are 9, 8, and 7, which add up to 24 — one more than the target of 23. Swapping the smallest of those (7) for the next digit down (6) gives 9 + 8 + 6 = 23, exactly the target. Arranged biggest to smallest: 9, 8, 6 → code "986".

Why this is the only answer

Systematically checking every set of three different digits from 1-9 that sums to 23 turns up exactly one: {9, 8, 6}. (9+8+7=24 is the maximum possible sum for three distinct 1-9 digits, so 23 — one less than the maximum — can only be reached by dropping the smallest digit by exactly 1, which forces this unique combination.) Verified by exhaustively checking all 3-digit combinations from 1-9 programmatically.

Source / fact used

Self-contained combinatorics/constraint puzzle — no outside lookup needed, only systematic checking of digit combinations.
