Internet Detective Academy · Case File

The Maestro's Missing Score

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 85-105 minutes (10 case files — grab a pencil for Case File 05)
Tonight, the Royal Concertgebouw in Amsterdam holds a special anniversary concert. An archivist keeps old papers and documents safe — and tonight, archivist Mr. de Wit is in trouble. The hand-written program from opening night, 1888, has vanished from its display case. He has left clues all over the building. Follow them, Agent, and bring the missing pages back before the curtain rises.
MISSION 01
EASY

The Telegram From 1888

Pinned above Mr. de Wit's empty display case is a note: "I was inspired by a building I once saw in Leipzig. Find out who designed these halls, and you're on the right track."

Search online: who was the architect who designed the Royal Concertgebouw building in Amsterdam? Type just his surname.

Hint 1

Try searching "Concertgebouw Amsterdam architect" — an official history page will name him.

💡 Did you know? The Concertgebouw opened on 11 April 1888, with an opening-night concert so big it needed 120 musicians AND a 500-person choir!

MISSION 02
EASY

The Apprentice's Word List

Taped beside the note is a half-finished Dutch practice list. Every word takes "de" — except one, which takes "het" instead.

Find the odd one out: de viool, de piano, de gitaar, het orkest, de trom. Type just that Dutch word, without "de" or "het" in front.

Agent's Notebook: stuck to the bottom of the list is a sticky note in Mr. de Wit's handwriting — "46 composer surnames are carved into the walls of the Grote Zaal. I always found that number useful to remember." Jot that number down before you move on.

Hint 1

Say each word out loud with "de" in front — for one of them, it will sound wrong. That one takes "het" instead.

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

The Grote Zaal's Missing Floor Plan

The Grote Zaal (Main Hall) is famous for its "shoebox" shape — a design shared by only two other concert halls on Earth.

Mr. de Wit's blueprint shows the rehearsal floor: a big rectangle with a small storage nook cut from one corner. Find the total floor area in square meters, so the movers know how much floor covering to order.

Every measurement you need is labeled right on the plan.
Hint 1

Try splitting the L-shaped floor into two simple rectangles, then add their areas together.

Hint 2

One rectangle is 18 m by 16 m. What are the width and height of the second piece?

MISSION 04
MEDIUM

Four Musicians, One Missing Chair

Backstage, four musicians are warming up: Anna, Bram, Chris, and Daan. Each plays in a different section — Strings, Brass, Woodwind, or Percussion.

Use these clues to work out who plays the woodwinds:

1. Anna does not play a brass or a string instrument.
2. Chris's instrument has a shiny brass mouthpiece.
3. Daan practices in the back row with the big kettledrums (large bowl-shaped drums).
4. The one musician left — who is not Anna, Chris, or Daan — plays a string instrument.

Type the name of the woodwind player.

Hint 1

Start with the clues that name an instrument feature directly — two of the four clues solve a musician right away.

Hint 2

Once you know who plays brass and who plays percussion, clue 4 hands you a third musician for free. Only one name and one instrument are left over — that's your woodwind player.

MISSION 05
MEDIUM

Two Suppliers, One Smart Choice

The archive needs 24 sheets of special paper to restore the damaged program pages. Grab a pencil for this one — you'll need to work out the TOTAL cost from each supplier, not just the price per sheet.

Papierhuis charges €3.25 per sheet. Shipping is free once your order passes €50.

ArchiefShop charges €2.75 per sheet — cheaper per sheet! — but adds a flat €18 shipping fee no matter how big the order is.

Work out the total cost for 24 sheets from each supplier. Which one actually costs less in total? Type that supplier's name.

Hint 1

Multiply 24 by each price per sheet first. Then add shipping — remember, one of the two might not even need to pay it.

Hint 2

24 × €3.25 = €78 for Papierhuis, and that order easily passes €50, so shipping is free. Now work out ArchiefShop's total and compare.

💡 Did you know? Only three concert halls built before 1901 are ranked among the best-sounding in the world for music — in Amsterdam, Vienna, and Boston. All three share that same "shoebox" shape!

MISSION 06
HARD

The Strip Beneath the Podium

Taped under the conductor's podium is a strip of dots and dashes — Morse code, a way of sending letters as short and long beeps.

Search online for a Morse code chart, then decode this message. It names the famous square where the Concertgebouw stands.

-- ..- ... . ..- -- .--. .-.. . .. -.
Hint 1

Each group of dots and dashes, separated by a space, is one letter. Decode them one at a time.

Hint 2

The message is one word, 11 letters long — the name of the famous square outside the concert hall.

MISSION 07
HARD

Two Stagehands, One Fib

Two stagehands, Femke and Joost, were the last people near the archive room. One of them always tells the truth. The other always fibs (never tells the truth) — but you don't know which is which yet.

Femke says: "Joost and I are both fibbing about tonight."

Only one answer to "who is fibbing?" keeps Femke's own sentence from contradicting itself. Work it out with pure logic — no searching needed. Type the name of the stagehand who is fibbing.

Hint 1

Try assuming Femke is telling the truth. Does her own sentence still make sense if that's true? If not, she must be the one fibbing.

Hint 2

If Femke is fibbing, her sentence "we are both fibbing" is false — so it is NOT true that both are fibbing. Since Femke is fibbing, that leaves only one possibility for Joost.

💡 Did you know? The composer Gustav Mahler called Amsterdam his "second musical home" after conducting the Concertgebouw Orchestra there many times between 1903 and 1911.

MISSION 08
HARD

Three Halls, One Coded Note

Stage 1 of 2

Only three concert halls built before 1901 are praised most for their acoustics (how good a room sounds for music) — one is the Concertgebouw itself, in the Netherlands. Search online: which two other countries are home to the remaining two halls on that famous list — and which of those two is in North America?

Type just that country's short name (for example: USA).

Hint 1

Search "world's best acoustics concert halls before 1901" — the three halls and their cities will be named together.

Stage 2 of 2

Mr. de Wit's final note is coded. To hide it, he shifted every letter FORWARD through the alphabet — by exactly as many places as there are letters in your Stage 1 answer.

To read his note, shift each letter of the coded message BACKWARD by that same number of places.

SRGLXP GUDZHU
Hint 1

Count the letters in your Stage 1 answer — that is your shift number.

Hint 2

S shifted backward becomes P. Try the same backward shift on every letter, keeping the space between the two words.

BIG "AHA!" MOMENT
AHA!

The Drawer's Two-Digit Code

You found it — a small drawer inside the podium, exactly where Stage 2 pointed. It needs a two-digit code to open.

A riddle is taped to the drawer: "IF you remember the number of composer surnames carved into the Grote Zaal's walls, THEN double that number, and subtract 12. That is your code."

Type the two-digit code.

Hint 1

You don't need to search for this one — you already wrote the number down, back in Mission 02.

Hint 2

Double 46 first, then subtract 12 from that result.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

The Maestro's Final Cipher

The drawer opens — the missing pages are safe inside! Mr. de Wit smiles and hands you one last note, written only in numbers:

4-9-19-3-15-22-5-18-25

"Each number is a letter's place in the alphabet," he says. "A is 1, B is 2, and so on. Decode it, and you'll have named tonight's real treasure."

Hint 1

Each number stands for a letter's position in the alphabet: A=1, B=2, C=3, and so on.

Hint 2

The word has 9 letters — and it's exactly what this whole case has been about: uncovering something hidden.

Case closed?

Case closed, Agent! Tonight's concert can begin, program pages and all — because you never stopped searching, decoding, and connecting clues most people would miss. That habit of chasing curiosity until it becomes an answer is called discovery, and it's yours to keep.
