Mission 04 · Medium · Technology & Computers

The Tiny Square That Held (Almost) Nothing

Answer: 3
How to get there

A standard 3.5-inch high-density floppy disk (the format shown in the photo, standard from the 1990s through the early-mid 2000s) holds 1.44 MB. A modern phone photo is given as roughly 4 MB. 4 ÷ 1.44 ≈ 2.78, which must be rounded UP to 3 whole disks, since a file can't be split across a fractional disk in practice — you need a third, only-partially-full disk to hold the remainder.

Why this is the only answer

1.44 MB is the well-established, essentially universal capacity for this exact disk format (it's derived from a fixed geometry: 80 tracks × 18 sectors × 512 bytes × 2 sides = 1,474,560 bytes ≈ 1.44 MB), so there's no other defensible capacity figure for a standard 3.5" HD floppy. Given a fixed 4 MB target, only one whole-number answer (3) results from rounding the division up.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search that the standard 3.5-inch HD floppy disk capacity is 1.44 MB (1,474,560 bytes), consistent across independent tech-reference sources.

Mission 05 · Medium · Dutch Language

Fleur's Postcard

Answer: het plein
How to get there

Dutch nouns take either "de" (common gender — the majority of nouns, including "de school" and "de klas") or "het" (neuter — a smaller group). "Plein" (square/yard) is a neuter noun, so it takes "het": "het plein." The child should type it lowercase, as one two-word phrase with a single space.

Why this is the only answer

Dutch articles are fixed per noun (memorized/looked up, not derived from spelling rules) — "plein" is unambiguously a het-word in every standard Dutch dictionary, so "de plein" would be grammatically incorrect.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search against Dutch dictionary sources (woorden.org and the ANW Algemeen Nederlands Woordenboek), which both confirm "plein" is neuter/onzijdig and takes "het." "School" was cross-checked as a de-word (common gender) via the same method, so the surrounding note text is grammatically correct as written.

Mission 08 · Hard · Mathematics (pen-and-paper elapsed time)

Twenty-Five Years, Give or Take a Few Days

Answer: 9191
How to get there

From June 15, 2001 to June 15, 2026 is exactly 25 years. Leap years in that span (years divisible by 4, none of which are century-non-leap exceptions here): 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024 — six leap days. So June 15, 2001 to June 15, 2026 = (25 × 365) + 6 = 9125 + 6 = 9131 days. Then add the remaining days from June 15 to August 14, 2026: 15 days left in June (30 − 15) + 31 days in July + 14 days in August = 60 days. Total: 9131 + 60 = 9191 days.

Why this is the only answer

Both endpoints are fixed, real calendar dates given directly in the puzzle, and the day-count between any two fixed calendar dates is a single determinate number. Verified independently with a script (Python's date arithmetic) as a cross-check: 9191 days, matching the manual calculation exactly.

Source / fact used

Pure calendar arithmetic from the two dates stated in the puzzle text; double-checked with an independent date-library calculation before publishing.

Mission 09 · Big "Aha!" · Multi-stage · Mathematics / Real-World Decision-Making

The Box Inside the Box

Stage 1 answer
9.43

How to get there: A real online inflation calculator based on official U.S. CPI data (e.g. in2013dollars.com, which cites U.S. Department of Labor CPI figures) shows that $5.00 from 2001 has the same buying power as approximately $9.43 in 2026 — a cumulative inflation of about 88.6% over 25 years. Why this is the only answer: this is a real, currently-published figure from an official-data-based calculator, not an invented rate — any calculator using the same CPI dataset and the same 2001→2026 date range should land within a few cents of $9.43 (inflation calculators refresh monthly as new CPI data is published, so a small amount of drift, e.g. $9.30–$9.55, is expected and should be accepted as correct if your child cites a real calculator and shows their search). The real-world decision: the $5 bill sitting untouched in the capsule is still worth exactly $5 today — but it would take $9.43 to buy what $5 bought in 2001. In real (inflation-adjusted) terms, that saved cash actually LOST a significant chunk of its buying power by just sitting still with no interest. This is the intended takeaway: cash alone doesn't grow — only interest or investment growth can outpace inflation. Source: in2013dollars.com CPI-based inflation calculator, cross-checked against general U.S. CPI trend data (2025 inflation ~2.7%, January 2026 ~2.4% year-over-year, consistent with a multi-decade cumulative figure in the high-80% range).

Stage 2 (final) answer
52

How to get there: Take Stage 1's answer, $9.43, and add its whole-dollar part (9) to its cents part (43): 9 + 43 = 52. Why this is the only answer: this stage is only solvable once Stage 1's exact figure is known — a different (even close) Stage 1 answer produces a different Stage 2 sum, so the dependency is genuine. Source: arithmetic on Stage 1's verified result.

The Last Code

Answer: wisdom
How to get there

The shift amount is 24 — the number of classmates who signed the letter in Mission 02 (this is the third and final time that number matters in this mission set). Shifting each ciphertext letter backward by 24 places is mathematically identical to shifting it forward by 2 places (since 26 − 24 = 2), which is much easier to do by hand:

U→W, G→I, Q→S, B→D, M→O, K→M, spelling WISDOM.

Why this is the only answer

The shift amount is fixed by a fact established earlier in the mission set (24), and a Caesar shift by a fixed amount produces exactly one decoded message. Verified by encoding "WISDOM" with a +24 shift by hand (W→U, I→G, S→Q, D→B, O→M, M→K = UGQBMK) to confirm the cipher and its decode are mutually consistent.
