Internet Detective Academy · Case File

The Deep-Sea Glow Mystery

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 75-95 minutes

You've been called aboard the research submersible Nautilus Wells. Its crew studies creatures that glow in the deep, dark ocean. But something is wrong: their light-signal logs — records of every flash and glow the sub's cameras catch — have strange gaps in them, as if something keeps interrupting the recordings.

The crew needs a sharp-eyed detective to study the data, decode the signals, and figure out what's really happening down in the dark. Solve every case file below, and the final clue will reveal the one quality every great ocean explorer needs most.

MISSION 01
EASY

The Glowing Fishing Rod

The crew shows you footage of an anglerfish. It hunts by dangling a glowing lure in front of its mouth, like a fishing rod with a light on the end. Fish swim up to the light — and straight into its jaws.

Search online to find out: what is the special one-word name for the glowing tip at the very end of the anglerfish's lure — the part that actually makes the light?

Hint 1

Try searching "anglerfish lure glowing tip name." The light comes from tiny glowing bacteria living inside that one part.

💡 Did you know? About 80% of the animals living between 200 and 1,000 meters deep make their own light. Down there, glowing — not sunlight — is the normal way to see and be seen.

MISSION 02
EASY

Real Glow or Just a Reflection?

Not everything that looks like it's glowing actually makes its own light. Some things just bounce light that's already there — that's called reflecting. Only true bioluminescence means an animal makes its own light, usually with a chemical reaction inside its body.

Look at these five things the crew logged tonight:

  1. A vampire squid's arm-tips flashing blue light in the pitch-black water.
  2. Moonlight bouncing off the surface of the sea.
  3. A comb jelly's rainbow-colored ripples when sunlight hits its rows of tiny swimming combs.
  4. An anglerfish's lure glowing on its own in the dark, far below any sunlight.
  5. A diver's flashlight beam bouncing off a fish's shiny silver scales.

How many of these five things truly make their own light (real bioluminescence), instead of just reflecting light that's already there?

📓 Dive Log — worth remembering: tonight's readout shows the vampire squid was spotted at 712 meters down. Jot that down, Agent — it might matter later.
Hint 1

Ask yourself for each one: would it still glow if every light in the world switched off? Only true light-makers would.

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

How Low Can You Go?

The crew jokes that their sub has only scratched the surface of how deep the ocean really goes. To prove their point, they want you to look up the deepest spot ever measured in any ocean on Earth.

Search online for Challenger Deep, the deepest known point in the Mariana Trench. Find its depth in meters, then round it to the nearest whole kilometer.

Hint 1

Search "Challenger Deep depth meters." Most reliable sources agree on a number just under 11,000 meters. Remember: 1 kilometer = 1,000 meters.

MISSION 04
MEDIUM

The Diver's Depth Chart

The crew keeps a chart of the ocean's four main depth zones, from the sunny surface down to the deep, cold bottom. Some numbers on their chart got smudged by seawater. Use the ones that are still readable to work out the missing measurement.

Use the depths marked on the left and the Midnight zone's thickness to find how thick the Abyssal zone is.
Hint 1

The Midnight zone starts at 1,000 m and is 3,000 m thick — so where does it end? The Abyssal zone runs from there down to 6,000 m.

💡 Did you know? More than 80% of Earth's ocean has never been mapped, observed, or explored by humans. There's a whole world of glowing creatures we haven't even met yet.

MISSION 05
MEDIUM

The Crew's Dutch Notebook

One of the sub's crew members keeps her dive notes in Dutch. Tonight she wrote:

"___ licht van de inktvis was blauw-groen."
(In English: "The squid's light was blue-green.")

Which word belongs in the blank — "De" or "Het"?

Hint 1

Think of another Dutch word for something you can't touch, like weather: "het weer." "Licht" follows the same pattern.

MISSION 06
HARD

Whose Report Can You Trust?

For one second, a glowing creature drifted past the porthole. Two crew members saw it — but they don't agree on what it was.

Mia says: "It had a long glowing lure, like a fishing rod. It must have been the anglerfish!"

Sam says: "It had eight long arms, all webbed together. It must have been the vampire squid!"

The sub's camera caught one clear detail: the creature had arms, not fins.

On this crew, one person always reports exactly what they saw. The other one guesses, and guessed wrong tonight. Which creature did the crew actually see?

Hint 1

An anglerfish is a fish. Think about what body parts a fish has — and doesn't have.

Hint 2

Whichever crew member named the creature that matches the camera's "arms" detail is the one telling the truth.

MISSION 07
HARD

Eyes in the Dark

No sunlight reaches the Midnight zone, so how does a submersible "see" anything at all down there? Crews use sonar — sound waves that bounce off objects and echo back, letting the sub build a picture of what's around it, even in total darkness.

Search online for the famous real research submersible Alvin, built and run by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI). What is the maximum depth, in meters, that Alvin is rated to dive to?

Hint 1

Try searching "Alvin submersible depth rating meters WHOI." The WHOI website states the exact number.

💡 Did you know? Almost no deep-sea creature can see red light — it disappears within the first few meters of water. But one fish, the dragonfish, can make and see red light, using it like a secret flashlight its prey can't detect.

MISSION 08
HARD

Racing the Clock to the Surface

The sub must be back at the dock by 17:45 today. Work backward from that time to find out when the ascent must begin.

Every ascent follows these fixed steps, in order:

  • Walk from the sub to the dock building: 10 minutes
  • Surface procedures once the sub is topside: 15 minutes
  • Ascent from the safety-stop depth to the surface: 20 minutes
  • Safety-stop pause: 30 minutes
  • Ascent time up to the safety-stop, which depends on which zone the sub starts from tonight:
    Twilight zone: 25 minutes · Midnight zone: 55 minutes · Abyssal zone: 80 minutes

Tonight's ascent starts from whichever zone the vampire squid sighting depth falls into — grab a pencil and work backward from 17:45 through every step above to find the exact clock time (24-hour format, like 16:05) the pilot must begin the ascent.

Hint 1

Start at 17:45 and subtract each listed step one at a time, working backward toward the start of the dive.

Hint 2

Before you can pick the right ascent-time number, you'll need to know which zone matches the depth from your earlier notes — check the depth chart from Mission 04.

MISSION 09
BIG AHA

The Signal Behind the Gaps

Stage 1 of 2

Grab a pencil for this one. Water pressure builds up fast the deeper you go: for every 10 meters down, the pressure grows by 1 more atmosphere (atm) — on top of the 1 atm already pressing down at the surface.

Tonight's dive log recorded five readings. Work out the pressure, in atm, for each one using the rule pressure = 1 + (depth ÷ 10), then find the average pressure across all five dives.

Stage 2 of 2

The gaps in the light-signal log finally make sense — something was crawling right in front of the sensor. Count the average pressure number you just found as a letter's position in the alphabet (A=1, B=2, C=3...). That letter is the FIRST letter of the culprit's name.

Decode the rest of the name using this light-flash (Morse code) signal, one group of dots and dashes per letter:

...   ---   .--.   ---   -..

Put your alphabet letter in front of the decoded letters to reveal the culprit's full name.

Hint 1

Use a Morse code chart. Each group separated by a space is one letter — decode them in order.

Hint 2

Counting A=1, B=2, C=3... which letter sits at your Stage 1 number?

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

The Truth About the Kraken

Case closed on the signal gaps — but now the dock crew is buzzing with rumors. One sailor swears giant squid can grow up to 60 feet (18 meters) long, a real-life "Kraken." Another crew member says that number is wildly exaggerated.

Search for what a trustworthy, science-based source — like the Smithsonian — actually confirms: what is the longest giant squid ever measured and recorded, in feet?

Hint 1

Search "Smithsonian giant squid longest recorded length feet" — trustworthy sources report a real measured number, not an internet legend.

Hint 2

The "60 feet" myth mixes up the giant squid's very long feeding tentacles with wild exaggeration. The true record is smaller.

Case closed?

You never just believed the rumor — you checked it against a real source before deciding what was true. That habit has a name: curiosity. It's what turned you from a passenger on this sub into a real deep-sea detective. Case file closed.
