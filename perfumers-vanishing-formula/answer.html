Mission 01 · Easy · Geography & World Knowledge

Where in the World Is Grasse?

Answer: France
How to get there

Search "Grasse perfume capital" or "Grasse France." Grasse is a real town in the Alpes-Maritimes department, Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur region, in southeastern France, about 16 km inland from Cannes. It has genuinely been the historic center of the French (and arguably world) perfume industry for over 300 years, home to houses like Fragonard, Galimard, and Molinard.

Why this is the only answer

Grasse is unambiguously a French town — there is no other country with a town of this name that fits the "perfume capital" description, and the "perfume capital" framing itself is widely and independently corroborated (travel guides, NPR, France's official tourism site), not an invented nickname.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search: multiple independent sources (NPR "Grasse perfume France," France.fr, Côte d'Azur tourism board, mapsofworld.com) confirm Grasse's location in the Alpes-Maritimes/Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur region of France and its reputation as the world's perfume capital.

Mission 02 · Easy · Dutch Language

Odette's Dutch Notebook Habit

Answer: flesje
How to get there

Check each word's article: de geur (scent), de bloem (flower), de jasmijn (jasmine), de roos (rose) — all four use "de" (common gender). "Flesje" is a diminutive form of "fles" (bottle); Dutch diminutives formed with -je/-tje are always neuter, so it takes "het": het flesje.

Why this is the only answer

Only one of the five words breaks the "de" pattern. The diminutive rule (-je endings are always "het") is a fixed grammar rule, not a judgment call, so "flesje" is the single unambiguous odd one out.

Source / fact used

Verified each word individually via web search against Dutch dictionary/grammar sources: Woorden.org and Dutch grammar references confirm "de geur," "de bloem," "de roos," and "de jasmijn" (common gender), and confirm the diminutive suffix -je is systematically neuter, giving "het flesje."

Mission 03 · Medium · Science & Nature / Logical Reasoning (Foundational — classification)

Four Flowers, One Fake Ingredient

Answer: tulip
How to get there

Jasmine, rose, and lavender are all real, extensively documented perfumery ingredients (jasmine and rose absolutes are literally produced in and around Grasse; lavender is a staple across colognes and soaps). Tulips, by contrast, have such a faint natural scent that almost no usable essential oil can be extracted from them — the child should notice Card 4 is the only one that describes a flower that does NOT genuinely yield perfume oil.

Why this is the only answer

Three of the four cards explicitly describe oil/extraction used in real perfumes; only the tulip's card describes the opposite (almost no extractable scent, synthetic recreations instead). No other flower on the list fits that description.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search: Wikiparfum and Fragrantica confirm jasmine and rose (especially Rosa centifolia from Grasse) as classic perfumery ingredients; multiple fragrance-industry sources confirm lavender's long use in perfumery/cologne. Multiple sources (Shay & Blue, Première Peau) independently confirm tulip's very low natural fragrance yield and reliance on synthetic reconstruction. The "10,600 jasmine flowers" detail on Card 1 is verified below (see Mission 05 fact check).

Mission 04 · Medium · Mathematics (pen-and-paper)

The Flask Ratio

Answer: 72
How to get there

Total flask = 150 ml. Subtract the 30 ml alcohol base (not part of the ratio): 150 − 30 = 120 ml remaining. That 120 ml is split 2 parts jasmine : 3 parts rose = 5 total parts. 120 ÷ 5 = 24 ml per part. Rose = 3 parts × 24 ml = 72 ml (jasmine = 2 × 24 = 48 ml, and 72 + 48 = 120 ✓, plus the 30 ml base = 150 ml ✓).

Why this is the only answer

The numbers are fixed and the ratio is fully specified (2:3 of a known 120 ml remainder), so there is exactly one arithmetic result. Recomputed independently to confirm: 150−30=120; 120/5=24; 24×3=72.

Source / fact used

Self-contained arithmetic puzzle; no external fact needed. Numbers were re-verified by hand before publishing.

Mission 05 · Medium · Cryptography (scent-wheel color cipher)

Odette's Scent-Wheel Code

Answer: amber
How to get there

Decoding the five dots left to right using the color key: Rose Pink = A, Golden Amber = M, Deep Violet = B, Leaf Green = E, Ruby Red = R. That spells A-M-B-E-R.

Why this is the only answer

Each color in the key maps to exactly one letter, and the five-dot sequence maps to exactly one five-letter string. There is no other valid decoding.

Source / fact used

"Amber" is a real, well-documented perfume note/accord (a warm, resinous blend typically built from labdanum, benzoin, and vanilla) — verified via web search (Fragrantica, The Perfume Society, Experimental Perfume Club) so the decoded word is a genuine, meaningful perfumery term, not an arbitrary string.

Mission 08 · Hard · Multi-Stage (History + Critical Thinking/Media Literacy)

Checking the Museum's Story

Stage 1 answer: 1747

Galimard, one of the oldest perfume houses in Grasse, was founded in 1747 by Jean de Galimard. This is stated directly on Galimard's own official website ("Founded in 1747") and corroborated by Wikipedia's article on Galimard. This is a solidly documented company-founding date, deliberately used instead of the popular but harder-to-verify "Catherine de Médicis and scented gloves" story.

Stage 2 (final) answer: A

How to get there: Site A quotes Galimard's founding year as 1747, matching the Stage 1 fact exactly. Site B quotes 1847 — a full century off from the verified date. Because Stage 1 already established 1747 as correct, Site B's date is demonstrably wrong, which is the giveaway that it isn't the genuine museum source (reinforced by its generic marketing tone and gift-shop-discount pitch, versus Site A's plain, institutional description as the City of Grasse's museum network).

Why this is the only answer: Site B's factual error is only catchable once Stage 1's real answer (1747) is known — this is what makes Stage 2 genuinely dependent on Stage 1 rather than solvable on its own.

Source / fact used: The real official website for Grasse's museums, including the Musée International de la Parfumerie (MIP), is museesdegrasse.com, run by the City of Grasse's own museum network ("Musées de Grasse") — confirmed via web search across multiple independent tourism and museum-listing sources (Côte d'Azur Tourism, France.fr, Pays de Grasse Tourisme, Ville de Grasse). Fact check · between Missions 08 & 09

"Did you know?" — Grasse's May Rose

Verified via web search: Rosa centifolia, grown around Grasse and nicknamed "Rose de Mai" (May Rose), is widely cited (Wikiparfum, Lampoon Magazine, historyofperfume.net) as producing some of the world's finest rose absolute, even though roughly 70% of the world's rose oil actually comes from Bulgaria, with smaller amounts from Turkey, Iran, Morocco, and Grasse.

Mission 09 · Big "Aha!" · Logical Reasoning (Advanced — formal connectives AND/OR/XOR)

The Big "Aha!" — Cracking the Real Formula

Answer: Bergamot, Jasmine, Lavender
How to get there

Rule 3 removes Vetiver immediately, leaving Jasmine, Rose, Lavender, Bergamot as candidates, of which exactly 3 are used. Rule 1 says Jasmine and Bergamot are both-in or both-out. Test "both out": then Rule 4 is automatically satisfied (no constraint), and Rule 2 needs exactly one of Rose/Lavender — but that only gives 1 total ingredient (Vetiver is out, Jasmine/Bergamot are out, only one of Rose/Lavender is in), which contradicts "exactly three." So Jasmine and Bergamot must both be IN. Rule 4 then forces Rose OUT (since Jasmine is in). Rule 2 (exactly one of Rose/Lavender) then forces Lavender IN (since Rose is out). Final set: Jasmine, Bergamot, Lavender — exactly three, matching every rule.

Why this is the only answer

Checked by testing all 10 possible 3-ingredient subsets of the 5 candidates against all four rules: only {Bergamot, Jasmine, Lavender} satisfies every rule simultaneously. Every other subset violates at least one rule (documented during red-team validation), confirming a unique forced solution.

Source / fact used

Self-contained logic puzzle. All five candidate notes (jasmine, rose, lavender, bergamot, vetiver) are real, verified perfumery ingredients (confirmed via web search against Fragrantica/Wikiparfum/industry sources), so the puzzle's setup is grounded in real perfumery even though the specific "formula" is fictional.

The Locked Case
