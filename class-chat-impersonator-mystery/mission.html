Internet Detective Academy · Case File

The Impersonator in the Class Chat

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 75-100 minutes (can be split across sessions)

Every week, the CircuitFoxes Robotics Club chats about parts, practice, and pizza in their private group chat — run by their coach, Coach Fenna, whose real account is always @Coach_Fenna. The club's real, official website is circuitfoxesclub.org.

Last night, something felt wrong: a message showed up that looked like it came from Coach Fenna, but it wasn't quite right. You've been called in as the club's Internet Safety Detective. Your job: figure out who — or what — is really behind it, spot every red flag before a single classmate clicks anything, and make sure the chat is safe again.

MISSION 01
EASY

The Weakest Lock in the Clubhouse

Before the scary message even shows up, Coach Fenna wants your help with something every club member should double-check: their backup security question, used if anyone ever gets locked out of the chat account. Here's what each member picked:

  • Kai — Question: "What is your favorite color?" Answer: Blue. (His profile picture is even a little blue robot.)
  • Lotte — Question: "What street did you grow up on?" Answer: Elm Street.
  • Finn — Question: "What was the name of your first stuffed animal?" Answer: Bartholomew.
  • Robin — Question: "What is your mother's maiden name?" Answer: de Groot.

A good security question should be hard for a total stranger to guess AND hard to look up. One of these four is dangerously weak — both easy to guess (there are only a handful of common answers) and easy to spot just by glancing at the person's public profile. Whose security question is the weakest?

Hint 1

For each question, ask yourself: about how many different answers are actually likely? And could a total stranger find the answer just by looking at their profile, without any real detective work?

💡 Did you know? Real password guidelines from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) say a password's LENGTH matters more than cramming in symbols and capital letters — a long, simple passphrase can actually be harder to crack than a short "complex" one.

MISSION 02
EASY

The Message From (Almost) Coach Fenna

Wednesday morning, this shows up in the CircuitFoxes group chat:

From: @Coach_Fenna_HQ 🚨 SUPER IMPORTANT — READ BEFORE MIDNIGHT!!! 🚨 CircuitFoxes has been specially chosen for a FREE robotics kit giveaway!! Only the FIRST members to claim it get one — tap here right now: circuitfoxesclub-prizes.win Don't tell anyone else in the chat, this offer is just for you! 💙🤖 Logged: Tuesday, 9:47 PM
🕵️ Agent's Notebook: Good detectives always log the exact time evidence appears. Write down: Tuesday, 9:47 PM — you never know when a timestamp will matter later in a case.

You already know the real coach's account is always @Coach_Fenna. Look very closely at who actually sent this message. The impersonator added one extra word onto the end of the real handle to make it sound official. Type just that one extra word.

Hint 1

Compare the sender name shown above, letter by letter, with @Coach_Fenna. What's different at the very end?

💡 Did you know? Tricks like this have a name: phishing. It's spelled with "ph" instead of "f" as a nod to 1970s hackers who called their phone tricks "phone phreaking" — phishing scammers "fish" for your information the exact same way, just online.

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

How Far Does One Click Travel?

Before the club figured out the message was fake, the scam had already started to spread. Grab a pencil for this one — it's much easier worked out on paper than in your head.

One nervous classmate, worried about missing out, forwarded the message to 3 friends outside the chat. Each of those 3 friends — not knowing any better — forwarded it to 3 more friends of their own. Then each of THOSE friends forwarded it one more time, to 3 more friends each, before anyone realized it was a scam.

Counting that very first classmate who forwarded it, how many people in total have now seen the scam link?

Hint 1

Draw it like a tree: one person forwards to 3, then each of those 3 forwards to 3 more, then each of those forwards to 3 more again. Add up every single person at every level — including the very first one.

MISSION 04
MEDIUM

Whose Password Left the Door Unlocked?

The message really did come from inside a real CircuitFoxes account — someone's login was used. But whose account was it, and how did the scammer get in? Coach Fenna gathered what she knows about each member's password habits:

  • Lotte set up two-factor authentication the day she joined — even with her password, nobody could log in without a second code sent to her mom's phone.
  • Finn uses a password manager that creates a brand new random password for every single site — none of his passwords are ever repeated anywhere.
  • Kai changed his password last month, right after Mission 1's warning — it's now 20 random characters long, and used nowhere else.
  • Robin loves how easy it is to remember just one password for everything — the very same one he's used since 3rd grade, on his old game website, his email, and the club chat.

Using process of elimination, whose account was almost certainly the way in?

Hint 1

Cross off anyone whose habit would have stopped a stranger from logging in, even if that stranger somehow got hold of one password.

MISSION 05
MEDIUM

De of Het? Coach Fenna Never Gets This Wrong

Coach Fenna always writes perfect Dutch, even in a quick chat message — it's kind of her thing. Here are four words that keep coming up in this case:

robot · club · wachtwoord · link

Three of these words use "de" (de robot, de club, de link...). Exactly ONE of them uses "het" instead. Which word is the odd one out?

Hint 1

Say each word out loud with "de" in front of it, and then with "het" in front of it. One combination will sound clearly wrong to a careful Dutch speaker like Coach Fenna.

MISSION 06
HARD

Four Addresses, One Real Website

Scammers usually can't use the club's exact real web address, so they build something that LOOKS almost the same. They might add extra words, swap a letter, or change the ending after the very last dot — even though ".org" and ".win" are two completely different websites, no matter what comes before them.

You know the club's real, official website is circuitfoxesclub.org. Here are four addresses that have been floating around the chat today:

  1. circuitfoxesclub.org
  2. circuitfoxesclub.org.prize-claim.net
  3. circuitfoxesclub-prizes.win
  4. circutfoxesclub.org

Only one of these is genuinely the club's real website. Type it exactly as shown above.

Hint 1

Compare each address letter by letter against "circuitfoxesclub.org" — the real one has no extra words, no extra dashes, and no missing or swapped letters.

Hint 2

For address #2, remember: what matters most is the ending right before the very last dot — everything written in front of that can be faked to look official.

💡 Did you know? A padlock icon or "https" in a web address only means the connection is encrypted — it does NOT mean the site is trustworthy. Security researchers at the Anti-Phishing Working Group found that more than 9 out of 10 phishing websites used https in 2023. Always check the actual address, not just the padlock.

MISSION 07
HARD

Where Was Everyone That Night?

You've proven Robin's account sent that message. But did Robin actually type it himself? Remember the exact time you logged from that very first suspicious message — you'll need it now, and it won't be repeated here.

Here's where each club member says they were that evening:

  • Robin's family locks all phones in a kitchen basket every night at 9:00 PM, no exceptions — confirmed by his older sister, who was doing homework in the kitchen the entire evening.
  • Lotte was on a plane to visit her grandparents, seatbelt sign on with no wifi, from takeoff at 8:15 PM to landing at 10:30 PM.
  • Finn was at swim practice from 8:30 PM to 10:00 PM, and phones aren't allowed anywhere near the pool deck.
  • Kai was on a family video call with his grandma from 9:15 PM to 10:00 PM, logged in the family's shared call history.

Check each alibi against the exact time you logged earlier. If your logic proves that NONE of the four club members could have personally sent that message at that exact time, type NOBODY.

Hint 1

Line up each alibi's time window against the timestamp you wrote down earlier. Does it cover that exact minute, or not?

Hint 2

If every single alibi covers that timestamp, what does that prove about all four of them at once?

MISSION 08
HARD

Crack the Fake "Verification Code"

Whoever is controlling Robin's account sends one more message: "To prove this is real, reply with this verification code!" — followed by a strange string of letters. Real verification codes are always random numbers, never actual words, which is a red flag all by itself. Let's read it anyway.

FRGH: WRWDOOB UHDO WUXVW PH

Every letter in this message has been shifted forward by the same number of places in the alphabet. Here's your crib: FRGH decodes to CODE.

Figure out the shift, then decode the rest of the message (shifting every letter backward by that same amount). Type the decoded message that comes after "CODE:" — the part written in capital letters.

Hint 1

F is 3 letters after C in the alphabet. R is 3 letters after O. Once you're sure of the shift, apply it to every remaining letter.

Hint 2

Shift every letter of the rest of the message backward by 3 places (so F becomes C, R becomes O, and so on).

MISSION 09
AHA!

How Did They Really Get In?

Stage 1 of 2

Time to work backward. You know Robin's account sent the message, and none of the four club members could have personally typed it. You also know Robin never once clicked a suspicious link or a password-reset request before this happened — and the scammer typed his EXACT password on the very first try, with no failed attempts ever logged.

If Robin never gave his password away and never clicked anything sketchy, where did the scammer most likely get an already-correct password on their very first try? Here's a hint toward the vocabulary: it's a two-word term for when a website's stored passwords get stolen and leaked onto the internet. Type your answer as two words.

Hint 1

Think back to Robin's password habit from Mission 4 — the same password on his old game site, his email, and the club chat. If that old game site were ever hacked, what would happen to everyone's saved passwords, including Robin's?

Stage 2 of 2

Even with a leaked password in hand, real accounts have a second lock that could have stopped the scammer cold — knowing the exact password wouldn't have been enough. Search the internet for the security feature that adds a second step to logging in, like a one-time code sent to your phone, on top of your password. It's usually shortened to a 3-character abbreviation. Type just the short abbreviation, not the full name.

Hint 1

The full name starts with "two-factor..." — search for what protects an account even after a password has already leaked.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

Case Closed: The Real Rule About Real Prizes

Stage 1 of 2

One thread is still loose: that "free robotics kit" the message promised. Nobody in the CircuitFoxes chat ever signed up for any giveaway or contest. Search the internet for real advice on how legitimate contests and sweepstakes actually work, from a trustworthy source like an official consumer-protection agency. Then answer this: can you legitimately win a prize for a contest you never entered? Type YES or NO.

Hint 1

Look for official consumer-protection advice on "prize scams" or "sweepstakes scams" — a trustworthy source like an official government consumer-advice site is a good place to start.

Stage 2 of 2 — Final Answer

Case wrap-up. Count every classic scam red flag you uncovered in this case: a copy-cat account handle, a countdown-style urgency, a lookalike web address, a fake "verification code" that was really just words, and a prize nobody ever entered to win. How many is that?

Now use that number as a shift, and decode this final message by shifting each letter BACKWARD that many places in the alphabet:

BNXITR

Type the decoded word — it's the value that guided every move you made today.

Hint 1

Count the red flags listed just above — there are exactly that many. That's your shift number.

Hint 2

Shift each letter of BNXITR backward by that many places (B becomes what letter? N becomes what letter?).

Case closed?

Robin's account is secured, the whole club has been warned, and not a single classmate clicked that link. Every red flag you spotted and every fact you double-checked before trusting it — that's real WISDOM in action. Case closed, Detective.
