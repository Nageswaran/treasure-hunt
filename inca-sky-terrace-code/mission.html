The Inca Sky-Terrace Code

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 75-100 minutes

A small earth tremor shook the Sacred District of Machu Picchu overnight, and a stone slab in one of the old storage rooms shifted — revealing something no one has touched in centuries. Lead archaeologist Dr. Elena Rivas has called in extra help before word gets out and the wrong people come looking.

You've been named Junior Field Detective for this dig. Using real history, sky-watching, terrace math, market money-sense, and the Incas' own knotted-cord code, can you find the true sealed chamber — and figure out exactly who to report it to first?

EASY

The Sun-Tying Stone

In the Sacred District stands a carved granite pillar called the Intihuatana — "hitching post of the sun" in Quechua, the Inca language. At the two solstices each year (the two days when the sun's path in the sky reaches its farthest point), its shadow behaves in opposite ways:

  • At the June solstice, the pillar casts its longest shadow, stretching south.
  • At the December solstice, it casts its shortest shadow, pointing north.

Today, your team measured the shadow at local noon: it stretched out long, toward the south. Which solstice matches this measurement — June or December? Type just the month.

Hint 1

Think about which solstice makes the shadow stretch out to its longest, not its shortest.

💡 Did you know? Machu Picchu's stone walls have no mortar at all — many blocks are cut so precisely that not even a knife blade fits between them.

The Final Stretch

Your team's tracker shows the final stretch of the Inca Trail: from the Sun Gate (Inti Punku) at about 2,720 meters, the trail drops down to Machu Picchu itself at 2,430 meters. This last stretch runs almost due southwest, according to the trail map.

Calculate the team's "waypoint code": take the elevation drop in meters between the Sun Gate and Machu Picchu, divide it by 10, then add the compass bearing (in degrees) for southwest — where north = 0°, east = 90°, south = 180°, west = 270°, and southwest sits exactly halfway between south and west. Type the final waypoint code number.

Hint 1

First find how many meters of elevation are lost between the Sun Gate and Machu Picchu — subtract the two elevations.

Hint 2

Southwest sits exactly halfway between south (180°) and west (270°) on a compass. Add that number of degrees to (elevation drop ÷ 10).

MISSION 07
HARD

The Knotted Cord

Long before paper reached the Andes, the Incas recorded numbers on quipu (also spelled khipu) — cords with knots tied at different points along their length. Real quipu used a base-10 place-value system, just like the numbers you use every day:

  • The position on the cord shows the place value: closer to the main cord means a bigger place (hundreds), further down means smaller (tens, then ones).
  • At the hundreds and tens positions, a tight cluster of single overhand knots shows the digit — count the knots in the cluster.
  • At the very last position (ones), a different knot is used instead: a long knot with one turn per unit, for digits 2 through 9 — or a special figure-eight knot to mean exactly 1.

One dangling cord that survived the tremor has three knot clusters, described above: 3 single knots closest to the main cord (hundreds), 7 single knots in the middle (tens), and a long knot with 4 turns at the very end (ones). What number does this cord record?

Hint 1

Work from the top of the cord down — the position closest to the main cord is the biggest place value (hundreds), and the very end of the cord is the smallest (ones).

Hint 2

3 single knots at the hundreds position = 300. 7 single knots at the tens position = 70. A long knot's turns work the same way at the ones position — 4 turns = 4. Add all three together.

💡 Did you know? Machu Picchu has more than 700 individual stone terraces. Their hidden layers of stone, gravel, and sand drained rain water and kept the mountainside from washing away.

Rooms: the Sun Temple, the Sacred Plaza, and the Storage Qolqa. Artifacts: a ceramic jug, a bronze knife, and a quipu cord.

  • Clue 2: Inés was working in the Storage Qolqa.
  • Clue 3: Tomás did not find the quipu cord.
  • Clue 4: The ceramic jug was found in the Sun Temple — and it wasn't Kwaku who found it.

Type the name of the team member who found the quipu cord.

Hint 1

Hint 2

Once you know Tomás found the jug in the Sun Temple, only two people and two artifacts are left. Clue 1 tells you which of those two can't have the bronze knife.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

The Wisdom to Report It Right

You've found it: a sealed chamber, undisturbed since long before Hiram Bingham's time. But finding it is only half the job — a real archaeologist never just keeps digging. Before your team opens the sealed doorway, you need to know exactly who to contact FIRST, right now, at the site.

You already know how this discovery will end, months from now: it will be catalogued and placed on permanent display at Peru's Larco Museum, in Lima. Dr. Rivas hands you three rules that got it there — work backward from that final museum case to figure out the very first person your team must contact today.

  • Rule 1: The Larco Museum only accepts artifacts that already carry an official transport permit from the Cusco Regional Culture Office.
  • Rule 2: The Cusco Regional Culture Office only issues a transport permit for finds that are already logged in Machu Picchu park's own site registry.
  • Rule 3: The park's site registry only logs a find after the on-duty park ranger has personally inspected and sealed it at the exact discovery spot.

Reasoning backward from the museum, who must your team radio FIRST, today, before anything else can happen? Type just one word.

Hint 1

Don't start from today and guess forward — start at the museum, the very last step, and ask what had to happen right before it.

Hint 2

The museum needs a permit from the Cusco office; the Cusco office needs the park's own registry entry; and the registry needs someone on-site to act first. Who is that?

Case closed?

Rule after rule, you traced the path backward until it led to one clear answer: call the ranger first, right now — not the office in Lima, not the museum, and not even Dr. Rivas's own supervisors far away. Respecting the proper order, instead of rushing straight to the biggest name, is exactly the kind of patient thinking real archaeologists prize. That patient, careful thinking is wisdom — and it's what protects Machu Picchu's secrets for the next detective who comes looking. Case closed, Agent.
