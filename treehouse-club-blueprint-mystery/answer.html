Mission 02 · Easy

The Right Tree for the Job

Answer: oak
How to get there

Real treehouse-building guides consistently recommend a mature, healthy hardwood tree (oak, maple, beech) with a thick trunk and strong branches. A young sapling is too thin and flexible to bear a platform's weight, and a dead tree has no living wood or root system holding it up — both are explicitly listed as trees to avoid.

Why this is the only answer

Of the three options given, only the mature healthy oak meets the "living, strong, thick-trunked hardwood" criteria that every real source agrees on; the sapling and the dead elm each fail on a different, well-documented safety reason.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search, e.g. Hunker — Treehouse Safety Rules and Recommendations and treelofts.com — How to Choose the Right Tree for Your Treehouse Project (both recommend a mature, healthy hardwood and warn against dead or very young trees).

How to get there

Why this is the only answer

All six side lengths on the diagram are consistent with a 5 m × 4 m outer rectangle minus a 2 m × 2 m notch (the diagram's own perimeter of 3+2+2+2+5+4 = 18 m matches the outer rectangle's perimeter of 2×(5+4) = 18 m, confirming the numbers agree), so there is exactly one shape and one area those measurements describe.

Why this is the only answer

Mission 08 · Hard

The Rope That Won't Slip

Answer: bowline
How to get there

The bowline knot forms a fixed loop at the end of a rope that does not slip or tighten under load, yet is still easy to untie afterward. It's one of the most widely taught knots for sailors, climbers, and rescue workers, and is commonly nicknamed the "king of knots."

Why this is the only answer

The clue describes a specific, well-documented combination of properties (fixed loop, doesn't slip/tighten, easy to release) plus the exact nickname "king of knots" — this combination points to one specific, famous knot, not a family of options.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search, e.g. Animated Knots by Grog — Bowline Knot and Boat-Ed — When to Use a Bowline Knot (both describe the bowline's non-slipping fixed loop and its "king of knots" reputation).

Mission 09 · Big Aha!

Lifting Without the Muscle

Answer: 3
How to get there

Mechanical advantage (MA) of a pulley system equals the number of rope segments directly supporting the load: MA = load ÷ effort. Here, load = 12 kg and the safe effort per kid = 4 kg, so MA needed = 12 ÷ 4 = 3. That means 3 supporting rope segments (a compound pulley arrangement) are required.

Why this is the only answer

The relationship "mechanical advantage = number of supporting rope segments = load ÷ effort" is a fixed physics formula, and the two numbers given (12 kg load, 4 kg safe effort) produce one exact, whole-number result with no rounding needed.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search, e.g. Study.com — How to Calculate the Mechanical Advantage of a Pulley and Firgelli Engineering — Pulley Mechanical Advantage Calculator (both confirm IMA = number of supporting rope segments = load/effort).
