Internet Detective Academy · Case File

The Drone Pilot's Missing Footage

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 75-95 minutes

At Chitwan National Park in Nepal, a team of wildlife rangers flies a small survey drone named Sky-Eye to watch over one of the world's rarest animals. Two mornings ago, Sky-Eye's camera locked onto something extraordinary near the Rapti River — and minutes later, its memory card and paper flight log both vanished from the ranger station. The chief ranger needs a sharp young detective to retrace Sky-Eye's last flight before the trail, and the animal's exact location, is lost for good. That detective is you.

MISSION 01
EASY

Sky-Eye's Silent Signal

Before you can retrace anything, you need to understand how Sky-Eye even knows where it is in the sky. Every survey drone carries a GPS receiver, which listens to signals from satellites far above Earth.

Search online to find out: at the very minimum, how many GPS satellites does a receiver need to lock onto at the same time to work out a full 3D position — its exact spot on the ground and its height above it? Type just the number.

Hint 1

A flat, 2D fix (just north-south and east-west) needs fewer satellites than a full 3D fix that also includes height. Search for the difference between a "2D fix" and a "3D fix."

MISSION 02
EASY

Sorting the Ranger Station's Word Cards

While you wait for the chief ranger, you notice six Dutch word cards pinned to a corkboard, each naming something at the drone station: drone, geheugenkaartje (memory card), camera, dier (animal), vleugel (wing), and signaal (signal).

Every Dutch noun takes either "de" or "het." Look up the correct lidwoord for each of these six words. How many of the six use "het"? Type just the number.

Hint 1

Diminutives — words ending in "-je" — are always het-words, no matter what the plain word normally takes. That's a free shortcut for one of these six.

💡 Did you know? GPS satellites orbit about 20,200 kilometers above Earth and zoom around the planet twice a day — one full loop every 11 hours and 58 minutes.

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

Whose Eyes Did Sky-Eye Catch?

Sky-Eye's very last clip shows a large animal standing in tall grass near the river, just before the signal cut out. The chief ranger's notes list three true facts about it:

1. It has only one horn on its nose — unlike some other rhino species, which have two.
2. Its thick grey skin has deep folds, making it look like it's wearing armor plates.
3. It's a plant-eater, and a full-grown male can weigh over 2,000 kilograms — nearly as heavy as an Asian elephant.

Four animals live in Chitwan National Park: the Bengal tiger, the Asian elephant, the gharial (a river crocodile), and the greater one-horned rhinoceros. Which one matches all three facts? Type its short, one-word nickname.

Agent's notebook Zooming in on the clip, you spot a battered metal collar around the animal's neck. Stamped on it: GPS COLLAR #418. Jot this down, Agent — a good detective never knows which detail will matter later.
Hint 1

Cross off any animal that doesn't match fact 1 first — that alone rules out two of the four right away.

💡 Did you know? A rhino's horn isn't made of bone at all — it's built from keratin, the very same protein that makes up your own hair and fingernails.

MISSION 04
MEDIUM

The Battery's Last Words

Right before it lost signal, Sky-Eye's onboard computer logged one final status word — in binary. Each block of 8 digits (called a "byte") stands for one letter, using the standard code computers use for text. Here's a decoder key for the letters you'll need:

A = 01000001 E = 01000101 L = 01001100 O = 01001111 S = 01010011 T = 01010100 W = 01010111

Now decode Sky-Eye's final message:

01001100 01001111 01010111

What was the drone's last battery status? Type the word.

Hint 1

Match each 8-digit block to the exact same 8 digits in the key above, one block at a time — there's no shortcut, just careful matching.

MISSION 05
MEDIUM

Retracing the Flight Path

Grab a pencil for this one. According to the launch log, Sky-Eye flew at a steady 42 kilometers per hour along the Rapti River for exactly 25 minutes before its signal disappeared.

How many kilometers did it travel in that time? Type just the number (decimals are fine, like 12.5).

Hint 1

First turn 25 minutes into a fraction of an hour (there are 60 minutes in an hour). Then multiply that fraction by the speed.

MISSION 06
HARD

Pinpointing the Last Known Signal

The ranger station's radio log recorded the last coordinates Sky-Eye pinged before it dropped off the map: 27.5747° N, 84.4936° E.

Type those numbers into Google Maps (or a similar real map site). What is the name of the village you land in — the main gateway town where jungle safaris into Chitwan National Park begin? Type just that village's name.

Hint 1

This village sits right on the Rapti River, on the eastern edge of the national park.

Hint 2

It's the same town most tourism websites about Chitwan mention first, as the starting point for elephant-back and jeep safaris.

💡 Did you know? The very first drones used to help protect rhinos from poachers in Nepal were tested right here in Chitwan National Park, back in June 2012.

MISSION 07
HARD

A Suspicious Message in the Inbox

While you're at it, the head ranger shows you an email that landed in the drone team's inbox this morning:

From: chitwan-support@techsupp0rt-nepal.net Subject: URGENT: Your Sky-Eye Drone Account Will Be Deleted in 1 Hour! Dear Ranger, we detected unusual activity on your Sky-Eye Drone Portal account. To avoid permanent deletion of your flight logs, reply with your username and password within 60 minutes, or click here to verify now.

Look closely at the sender's email address. One word in it has had a letter secretly swapped for a number, hoping nobody would notice. Type just that one word, exactly as it's written (letters and the number together, no spaces).

Hint 1

Real companies don't usually spell their own name with a number standing in for a letter. Read the part of the address right before "-nepal.net," one character at a time.

Hint 2

The word is meant to look like a common English word about helping customers — but a zero has taken the place of one letter.

MISSION 08
HARD

Four Pilots, One Missing Flight

Stage 1 of 2

Four rangers took turns piloting drones that morning: Anjali, Bikash, Chandra, and Deepak. Each flew a different drone — Falcon, Heron, Kite, or Osprey — and each launched at a different time: 06:00, 07:15, 08:30, or 09:45. Use these four clues to work out who flew what, and when:

1. Anjali flew the Falcon, and took off first, before all the other pilots.
2. Bikash took off last of all four pilots — right after Deepak.
3. Chandra did not fly the Heron, and took off before Deepak.
4. The Osprey lifted off exactly one time-slot after the Kite, and neither of those two drones was flown by Bikash.

Every pilot's ID badge has a number: Anjali = 14, Bikash = 53, Chandra = 28, Deepak = 39. The Heron was the drone that recorded Sky-Eye's final clip. What is the badge number of the pilot who flew the Heron? Type just the number.

Hint 1

Start with clues 1 and 2 — together they pin down every pilot's exact take-off time, with no guessing needed.

Hint 2

Once you know all four times, clue 4 tells you which two drones flew in neighboring time-slots. Only one arrangement of the remaining drones fits every time slot without breaking clue 2.

Stage 2 of 2 (final)

The memory card wasn't in anyone's pocket — it was locked inside one of 100 numbered supply lockers at the ranger station. A note taped to the cabinet explains how to find the right one: take the badge number you just found, subtract 19, then multiply what's left by 2.

Which locker number holds the memory card? Type just the number.

Hint 1

Do the subtraction first, then the multiplication — the order matters here.

BIG "AHA!" MOMENT
AHA!

Cracking the Autopilot's Rulebook

Inside the locker, next to the memory card, you find a torn page from Sky-Eye's autopilot manual. It explains that every automatic flight-path code is exactly 3 direction symbols long, chosen from N (North), S (South), E (East), and W (West). Three rules apply to every single code:

1. The FIRST symbol must be N or E — Sky-Eye always launches away from the ranger station, never south or west.
2. No direction can repeat immediately — the same letter can never appear twice in a row.
3. The LAST symbol can never be W — that would send the drone straight back over the river at the very end, which is against the rules.

How many different valid 3-symbol flight-path codes are possible, following all three rules at once? Type just the number.

Hint 1

List every code starting with N in an organized way, then do the same for every code starting with E. Two separate lists are easier to check than one big jumble.

Hint 2

For a code starting with N, there are 3 legal choices for the middle symbol. But the number of legal endings isn't the same for every one of those choices — check each branch on its own instead of assuming they match.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

The Flight Log's Final Hiding Place

The memory card is safe — but Sky-Eye's paper flight log, with the coordinates of every rhino sighting that season, is still missing. One last note, tucked inside the locker, reads:

"Add the locker number to the last two digits of the number stamped on the animal you found. That total is the shelf where I stashed the log for safekeeping."

Add those two numbers together. Which shelf number holds the flight log? Type just the number.

Hint 1

You already worked out the locker number in an earlier mission. The other number is still sitting in your notebook from a few missions back — nobody restated it for you on purpose.

Hint 2

From that earlier number, you only need its last two digits, not the whole thing.

Case closed?

Shelf found, log recovered — every rhino sighting that season is safe again, all because you kept following the trail even when the signal went quiet. The value that cracked this case is discovery: the thrill of finding what everyone else thought was lost for good.
