Mission 01 · Easy

The Notebook's Soggy Sorting Page

Answer: Canada Goose
How to get there

Check each of the five birds against both circles. Arctic Tern is strongly migratory but is a tern (family Laridae), not waterfowl — Migratory only. Mallard Ducks are waterfowl (Anatidae), but many populations — especially in temperate Europe — stay resident year-round rather than migrating — Waterfowl only. Ostrich is flightless, non-migratory, and not waterfowl — neither circle. European Robin is a partial migrant (many continental European birds fly south for winter) but is a small songbird, not waterfowl — Migratory only. Canada Goose is both a true waterfowl species (Anatidae) and a classic long-distance migrant between Canadian/Alaskan breeding grounds and wintering grounds farther south — it belongs in the overlap.

Why this is the only answer

Of the five, only Canada Goose is unambiguously both migratory and a member of the duck/goose/swan (waterfowl) family — every other bird fails at least one of the two conditions.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search against multiple sources, including Alaska Dept. of Fish & Game, Audubon Field Guide, and Cornell Lab of Ornithology (All About Birds) species pages for Canada Goose, Mallard, European Robin, Arctic Tern, and Ostrich.

Mission 03 · Medium

Which Way to Antarctica?

Answer: South
How to get there

Svalbard sits at roughly 78°N, deep in the Arctic. Antarctica's pack ice, where Arctic Terns winter, sits at the opposite end of the globe, around 60-70°S or further. Traveling from one pole's neighborhood to the other's is, in general compass terms, a journey south (the birds' actual route zig-zags along coastlines and ocean currents, but the overall direction of travel is south).

Why this is the only answer

Any answer other than "south" would put the bird moving toward the Arctic it just left, not toward Antarctica.

Source / fact used

Cornell Lab of Ornithology (All About Birds), National Wildlife Federation, and Cool Antarctica — all confirm Arctic Terns breed in the Arctic (including Svalbard) and winter at the edge of the Antarctic pack ice, flying south for the Southern Hemisphere summer.

Mission 04 · Medium

Mapping the Marsh by Hand

Answer: 110 hectares
How to get there

The diagram is a 6x4 grid (24 unit squares) with each of the four corners sliced off by a diagonal line through that corner square. That leaves 20 squares fully inside the shape, plus 4 corner squares that are each cut exactly in half by the diagonal (contributing 0.5 each). Total squares = 20 + (4 × 0.5) = 20 + 2 = 22 squares. At 5 hectares per square: 22 × 5 = 110 hectares. (Confirmed independently with the shoelace formula on the polygon's grid coordinates: area = 22 square units.)

Why this is the only answer

The diagram was built so every partial square is a clean half (a true diagonal cut through a unit square), so there is no genuine ambiguity in the count — a careful row-by-row count always lands on 22 squares, and 22 × 5 = 110.

Mission 05 · Medium

The Ranger's Braille Note

Answer: 24
How to get there

The three braille cells shown are: (1) the number sign, dots 3-4-5-6; (2) dots 1-2, which is the letter "b" — and "b" preceded by the number sign reads as digit 2; (3) dots 1-4-5, which is the letter "d" — and "d" preceded by the number sign reads as digit 4. Together: 2, then 4 → "24".

Why this is the only answer

Each 6-dot cell maps to exactly one letter in standard English Braille (grade 1); the number sign converts the first ten letters (a-j) to a fixed digit each (a=1 ... j=0), so this specific 3-cell sequence decodes to one and only one number.

Source / fact used

Standard English Braille (grade 1) letter-to-dot mapping, confirmed via web search summaries drawing on braille reference charts (e.g. National Braille Press alphabet card, Perkins School for the Blind, disabled-world.com braille conversion chart): number sign = dots 3-4-5-6, placed before letters a-j to yield digits 1-0; a=dot1, b=dots1-2, c=dots1-4, d=dots1-4-5, matching the standard, unchanging definition of English Braille grade 1.

Mission 08 · Hard

The Godwit Debate

Answer: Claim 2
How to get there

The Black-tailed Godwit is the national bird of the Netherlands, but its Dutch breeding population has fallen sharply over the last 50 years — from roughly 120,000 breeding pairs to about 25,000 — mainly due to agricultural intensification. It is currently listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN Red List, and conservation groups (including the EU and AEWA) have been calling for stronger protection, not less. Claim 1's "fully recovered, no longer needs protecting" framing is a plausible-sounding but false piece of good news; Claim 2 matches the verified, current status.

Why this is the only answer

Multiple independent, reputable sources (IUCN Red List status, BirdLife International, peer-reviewed conservation literature, and Dutch national news coverage of EU pressure on the Netherlands) all agree the population is still declining and the species remains Near Threatened — none support Claim 1's "problem solved" framing.

Source / fact used

IUCN Red List status (Near Threatened) and BirdLife International species factsheet for Limosa limosa; population decline figures (120,000 to ~25,000 breeding pairs over 50 years) corroborated by wadertales.wordpress.com and DutchNews.nl reporting on Dutch and EU conservation efforts.
