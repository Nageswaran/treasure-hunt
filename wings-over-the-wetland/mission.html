Internet Detective Academy · Case File

Wings Over the Wetland

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 75-95 minutes (10 missions — take breaks between them!)

A storm tore through Reedmoor Wetland Reserve last night — right as Dr. Sanne Vos, this year's visiting Global Bird Count coordinator, was finishing her plan for the big day. Wind ripped through the observation hut, and her notebook pages, maps, and photos blew everywhere. Today is the count: volunteers arrive at nine to help scientists track how birds are doing, all around the world, on the very same day. Without Dr. Vos's plan, the count can't run — and the reserve badly needs a good count to help win an important conservation grant.

Ranger Elin Broek found you first and made you a Junior Detective on the spot. Your job: piece the science plan back together, page by page, before the volunteers show up.

📋 Ranger's field note (tucked in the corner of a damp page): "Banding team ringed 12 birds near the reed bed this morning, before the storm — worth jotting down."
MISSION 01
EASY

The Notebook's Soggy Sorting Page

The first dry page you find is Dr. Vos's sorting chart. She was placing today's five most likely reserve visitors into two circles: one for birds that are MIGRATORY (they travel long distances between seasons) and one for WATERFOWL (ducks, geese, and swans). Some birds belong in just one circle. Some belong in neither. But exactly one clever bird belongs in both — right in the middle, where the circles overlap.

Her five candidates: Arctic Tern, Mallard Duck, Ostrich, European Robin, Canada Goose.

Search each bird's migration habits and its family (is it a duck/goose/swan, or something else?) before you sort.
Hint 1

Search "is a [bird name] migratory" and "is a [bird name] a waterfowl species" for each of the five — waterfowl means the duck, goose, and swan family specifically.

MISSION 02
EASY

The Photograph That Survived

The storm ruined the memory card in Dr. Vos's camera, but a torn field-note survived, taped inside her notebook: "...cap black as ink, tail forked like scissors, nests near the North Pole, flies farther every year than any other bird alive..." Search to find out which of the five birds from Mission 1's list matches every clue in that note.

Hint 1

Search for a bird with a solid black cap and a deeply forked tail. One species breeds near the North Pole and travels farther every year than any other bird on Earth.

💡 Did you know? The first scientific bird-ringing program began in 1899, when a Danish teacher named Hans Mortensen put numbered aluminium rings on starlings' legs to trace where they flew. Within twenty years, most of Europe had copied his idea.

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

Which Way to Antarctica?

That photo in Mission 2 was taken on Svalbard — a cluster of islands deep in the Arctic Ocean, and one of this bird's breeding grounds. Once its chicks can fly, this species sets off on the longest journey of any animal on Earth: all the way to the edge of the pack ice at the rim of Antarctica, to enjoy the southern summer before flying all the way back again.

Picture a compass rose sitting over Svalbard. In one word, which general compass direction does this bird fly to reach its Antarctic wintering grounds?

Hint 1

Find Svalbard and Antarctica on a world map or a globe. One sits at the very top of the world, the other at the very bottom.

Hint 2

You don't need the bird's exact zig-zagging path across the oceans — just think about which pole is behind it, and which pole is ahead.

MISSION 04
MEDIUM

Mapping the Marsh by Hand

Dr. Vos's map page shows the reserve's wetland outline traced over her survey grid. Each grid square stands for 5 hectares of land (about 5 sports fields). Grab a pencil for this one: count every full square inside the shape as 1. Each corner is sliced off in a clean diagonal cut, so count each of those triangle corners as about ½ a square. Add up your total number of squares, then multiply by 5.

Count carefully, row by row — it's easy to miss a corner triangle.
Hint 1

Try counting one row of the grid at a time, left to right, so you don't lose track of which squares you've already counted.

Hint 2

There are 20 squares that are completely covered by the shape, plus 4 corner triangles that are each half a square. Every partial square here is a clean half, so your total comes out to a whole number of squares — no rounding needed.

MISSION 05
MEDIUM

The Ranger's Braille Note

The reserve is testing new braille signs so every visitor can enjoy the trails. Ranger Broek left her observation post number written the braille way, on a torn notebook corner. Braille letters are made of raised dots in a 6-dot cell (2 columns, 3 rows). The first ten letters, a through j, double up as the digits 1 through 0 whenever a special "number sign" comes right before them. Here's the key:

Filled dot = raised. Open dot = flat (not part of the letter). The "#" cell is the number sign — it means "what follows is a number, not a letter."

Now decode Ranger Broek's post number, written as three braille cells in a row: a number sign, then two digit-letters.

Match each dot pattern to the chart above, letter by letter.
Hint 1

Compare each cell dot-by-dot against the reference chart — the number sign only tells you "these are digits," it isn't a digit itself.

Hint 2

The second cell matches the same pattern as digit 2. The third cell matches the same pattern as digit 4.

💡 Did you know? The Wadden Sea — which touches the coasts of the Netherlands, Germany, and Denmark — can hold up to 6.1 million birds at once, and 10 to 12 million pass through it every year on their way along the East Atlantic Flyway.

MISSION 06
HARD

Four Volunteers, One Morning

Dr. Vos's volunteer schedule blew apart into loose clues. Four volunteers — Priya, Tomas, Elena, and Marcus — each carried a different color of binoculars (green, black, red, blue), each spotted a different bird (Canada Goose, Black-tailed Godwit, Mallard Duck, European Robin), and each began watching at a different time (7:00, 8:30, 10:00, 11:30). Grab a pencil and draw yourself a 4x4 grid — this one's easier to track on paper. Use every clue below; each fact is true.

  1. The volunteer with green binoculars began watching at 7:00.
  2. Priya's binoculars are not black and not blue.
  3. Whoever spotted the Canada Goose began at the earliest time of the morning.
  4. Tomas began later than whoever spotted the Mallard Duck, but earlier than whoever carried blue binoculars.
  5. Elena's binoculars are neither green nor blue.
  6. The Black-tailed Godwit was spotted exactly 90 minutes after the Mallard Duck.
  7. Marcus carried blue binoculars.
  8. Whoever carried red binoculars started at 8:30 or 10:00 — never first, never last.
  9. Whoever spotted the Black-tailed Godwit was not carrying red binoculars.

Name the volunteer who was wearing black binoculars when they spotted the Black-tailed Godwit.

Hint 1

Start with the clues that name a person or a color directly (clues 2, 5, 7) before touching the time clues — they'll narrow your grid fastest.

Hint 2

Once you know the Canada Goose spotter's time (clue 3) and the green-binoculars person's time (clue 1), those two facts point to the same volunteer.

MISSION 07
HARD

Two Offers on the Table

Two conservation groups have offered to help fund new bird bands and trail signs. Ranger Broek needs your help choosing:

  • Offer A — Wetland Futures Fund: a flat grant of $650, no conditions.
  • Offer B — GreenPath Conservation Trust: matches every dollar visitors donate today, dollar for dollar, up to a cap of $900.

Today, visitors donate a total of $475. The reserve keeps its visitor donations either way — the offers only differ in what the grant itself adds on top. Work out the reserve's grand total under each offer (donations plus grant money), then find the difference. How many more dollars will the reserve end up with by picking Offer A instead of Offer B?

Hint 1

Work out the reserve's total money under each offer separately first — remember the $475 in donations counts toward the total both times.

Hint 2

Offer B only matches what was actually donated, up to its cap. Since $475 is less than the $900 cap, the match equals $475 exactly — not the full cap.

MISSION 08
HARD

The Godwit Debate

Two volunteers are arguing about the Black-tailed Godwit — the Netherlands' national bird, and a regular visitor to the reserve's marshes. Only one of them is right.

Claim 1: "Great news — the Black-tailed Godwit is now a common bird with a growing population across the Netherlands, so it doesn't need protecting anymore!"

Claim 2: "The Black-tailed Godwit population in the Netherlands has been shrinking for decades, and the species is now listed as Near Threatened."

A quick search of a trustworthy source — like the IUCN Red List or BirdLife International — confirms only one of these. Which claim is actually true?

Hint 1

Search for the Black-tailed Godwit's current conservation status on a reputable site like the IUCN Red List or BirdLife International, not a random blog.

Hint 2

Look for how the Dutch breeding population has changed over the last 50 years, not just how common the bird looks today.

💡 Did you know? A tagged Bar-tailed Godwit — a cousin of the Black-tailed Godwit — once flew nonstop from Alaska to New Zealand without landing, resting, or eating a single bite: well over 7,000 miles (over 11,000 km) in about a week and a half. Different tracked birds have set slightly different distance records as tracking tags improve, but each one is the longest nonstop flight ever recorded for a landbird.

MISSION 09
AHA!

The Reed Bed Recount

Stage 1 of 2

Before you calculate anything else, dig back through your notes from earlier this morning: how many birds did the banding team ring near the reed bed, before the storm hit?

Hint 1

It was written down early on, in a small note from Ranger Broek — not inside any of the numbered missions themselves.

Stage 2 of 2

Now it's early afternoon. The team sets a fresh net by the reed bed and catches 15 birds. Of those 15, 3 are already wearing a band from this morning. Real scientists use a trick called mark-recapture to estimate how many birds live in an area, without having to count every single one:

estimated population = (first count × second count) ÷ recaptured count

Using the number you found in Stage 1 as the "first count," and today's new catch as the "second count," estimate the total number of birds living in this part of the wetland.

Hint 1

Multiply first — the two numbers you multiply are your Stage 1 answer and 15. Then divide that result by 3.

Hint 2

This real technique is called the Lincoln-Petersen method. It works because the fraction of the second catch that's already banded should roughly match the fraction of the whole population that's already banded.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

Closing the Field Report

The very last clean line in Dr. Vos's notebook reads: "Global Bird Count Day doesn't run on fancy equipment. It runs on ______ — the simple urge to find out what's really going on." The last word is smudged, but its letters are still legible, just scrambled, at the bottom of the page:

I T R U C O Y S I

Unscramble the letters to complete Dr. Vos's sentence.

Hint 1

It's the feeling that makes you want to open every drawer, ask "why," and follow a clue all the way to the end — which is exactly what you've been doing all day.

Hint 2

It starts with the same sound as the word "curious."

Case closed?

Case closed, Junior Detective! Dr. Vos's storm-scattered plan is whole again, and the Global Bird Count can go ahead exactly on schedule. But the real discovery today wasn't the notebook — it was watching you follow clue after clue simply because you wanted to know the answer. That restless, wonderful itch to find out is called curiosity, and it's the very same thing that turns ordinary people into real citizen scientists, at count days just like this one, all over the world.
