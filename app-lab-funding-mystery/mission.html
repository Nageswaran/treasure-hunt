Internet Detective Academy · Case File

The App Lab Funding Mystery

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 60-75 minutes

Last night, the App Lab Coding Club held Demo Night — five student-built apps shown off to raise money for Fernwood Animal Shelter, with every donated dollar promised straight to the shelter. This morning, the amount actually in the cash box doesn't match last night's tally sheet, and the club has to hand the district's Innovation Grant Committee an exact, honest total at tomorrow's big pitch.

Club advisor Ms. Alvarez has called in the Internet Detective Academy's sharpest agent — you — to retrace every dollar from last night, figure out exactly what's missing, and crack open a locked overflow cash box before the deadline. Nobody's in trouble. You just need to follow the clues.

MISSION 01
EASY

The Closet Keypad

Taped inside Ms. Alvarez's planning notebook is a torn sticky note. Someone scribbled "SUPPLY CLOSET CODE (binary!)" above a string of 1s and 0s:

101011

The closet's keypad only understands regular decimal numbers, not binary. Convert the binary code above into a normal decimal number to open the closet — that's where last night's donation paperwork is stored.

Hint 1

Each digit stands for a power of two. Reading left to right, the six columns are worth 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, and 1 — add up only the columns where you see a 1.

MISSION 02
EASY

Two Sticker Shops

Inside the closet, you find quotes from two print shops the club was comparing for donor thank-you stickers. App Lab needs exactly 120 stickers for tomorrow's pitch.

ShopDeal
Bright Print Co.$16.00 per bundle of 40 stickers (bundles only)
QuickSticker Shop$0.30 per sticker, any amount

Work out the total cost of exactly 120 stickers at each shop. How many dollars would App Lab save by choosing the cheaper shop instead of the pricier one?

Type just the number of dollars saved — no dollar sign, no decimal point. Example: 12

Hint 1

Bright Print only sells in bundles of 40 — figure out exactly how many bundles equal 120 stickers, then find each shop's total price for 120.

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

The Gym Floor Plan

Demo Night was held in the school gym. The diagram below shows the gym floor from above — a big rectangle, with a smaller rectangular "stage alcove" roped off in one corner for the sound equipment (no donation booths allowed there).

Every side length you need is marked on the diagram. How much floor space (in square meters) is left over for donation booths, once the stage alcove is roped off?

Type just the number of square meters. Example: 50

Hint 1

Try splitting the whole floor into one big rectangle (as if the alcove weren't there), then subtract the area of the roped-off alcove rectangle.

💡 Did you know? The word "bit" (as in a binary digit, a 1 or a 0) was coined in 1947 by mathematician John Tukey at Bell Labs, as a short way of saying "binary digit." Every photo, app, and website your computer shows you is ultimately built from millions of these tiny bits!

MISSION 07
HARD

The Hand-Drawn QR Grid

App Lab's printer broke, so someone hand-drew a QR-style code for online donations and taped it to the jar. Real QR codes were invented in 1994 by an engineer named Masahiro Hara, at a Japanese company called Denso Wave, to help track auto parts moving through car factories — "QR" literally stands for "Quick Response." Denso Wave even chose not to enforce their patent, which is part of why anyone, even a school coding club, can put a QR code on a homemade poster for free.

Under the jar's grid, a note reads: "Read top row first, five blocks per row, dark = 1, light = 0." Each row is a 5-digit binary number.

Convert each row's 5-digit binary number to a decimal number (1-26), then to a letter using A=1, B=2, C=3 ... Z=26. Five rows, five letters, one word.

Type the word you spell, all lowercase.

Hint 1

Each row is one letter. Convert it the same way you did for the closet's binary code — add up the columns worth 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 wherever there's a dark block.

Hint 2

Real QR codes are scanned one small square at a time, in a set order — do the same here: solve Row 1 all the way to a letter, then Row 2, and so on, top to bottom.

💡 Did you know? Denso Wave, the Japanese company that invented the QR code in 1994, decided not to enforce its patent rights on the design. That single decision is a big part of why QR codes became free for anyone in the world to use — from grocery store receipts to school fundraiser posters.

MISSION 08
HARD

Who Has the Spare Key?

Behind the stage alcove sits the overflow cash box you've heard about — but it's locked, and Ms. Alvarez needs the spare key to even try opening it. Three club members answer when she asks who has it:

  • Sam: "I've got the spare key right here."
  • Ruby: "Sam does not have the key."
  • Theo: "I've got the spare key."

Exactly ONE of these three statements is mistaken (or a joke) — the other two are the plain truth. Who really has the spare key?

Hint 1

Sam and Theo can't both be telling the truth — they're each claiming to have the SAME single key. Figure out which one of the two of them is right first.

Hint 2

Try assuming Sam is the mistaken one, and check whether Ruby's and Theo's statements agree with each other without any contradiction. Then try assuming Theo is the mistaken one instead, and see what breaks.

MISSION 09
AHA!

The True Shortfall

Stage 1 of 2

One more receipt turns up, this time from the online donation app itself. It turns out the app quietly charges a $0.25 service fee on every single online gift — a fee that's subtracted before the money ever reaches the club's account. Nobody "lost" that money; it just never arrived in the first place, so it never showed up as missing before now.

The receipt shows the number of online gifts that came in last night — and it happens to match a number you already know by heart from earlier tonight, set aside in its own little envelope.

Multiply that number of gifts by the $0.25 fee, then add the result to the shortfall you calculated earlier. What is the TRUE amount still unaccounted for, in whole dollars?

It comes out to a whole number of dollars — no cents needed. Example: 40

Hint 1

Multiply the number on that little envelope by $0.25, then add that onto the shortfall amount you already worked out.

Stage 2 of 2

Ms. Alvarez remembers two things about the overflow cash box's 3-digit combination lock: the three digits add up to a number related to the true shortfall you just found, and the first digit is even.

Add the two digits of your Stage 1 answer together — that sum is the code's digit-sum. Type that digit-sum as your answer.

Hint 1

If your Stage 1 answer was a two-digit number, just add those two digits to each other.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

Cracking the Cash Box

You already know two things about the overflow box's 3-digit lock: the digits add up to the digit-sum you just found, and the first digit is even. That still leaves a lot of possible codes. Luckily, three club members each remember one more detail:

  • Maya: "I'm sure the first digit was a 4."
  • Jonas: "The second digit was bigger than the third digit."
  • Priya: "The third digit was even — but it definitely wasn't a zero."

Grab a pencil: list out every 3-digit code that starts with 4 and whose three digits add up to your digit-sum. Then use Jonas's and Priya's clues to cross options off, one by one, until exactly ONE code is left.

Picture spinning the dial to that code — the box clicks open. Inside is a strip of paper reading EPLXHZCV, with a note: "Shift every letter backward through the alphabet by the digit-sum you already know (wrap from A back around to Z if you need to)."

Decode the strip to reveal the word that describes exactly how App Lab cracked this case tonight. Type that word.

Hint 1

Start by listing every code that begins with 4 and whose three digits add up to your digit-sum — write them all out in a column, there aren't too many.

Hint 2

Cross off any code where the second digit ISN'T bigger than the third. Then, of what's left, cross off any code where the third digit is odd, or is a zero.

Case closed?

Nobody stole a thing — the money was scattered across a jar, an app, a check, and a stack of raffle stubs, and it took every single clue (and every club member's memory) working together to add it all back up. That's exactly what tomorrow's grant committee is going to hear about: teamwork is what actually saved Demo Night.
