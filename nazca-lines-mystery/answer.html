Mission 01 · Easy · Part 1: Geography

Where in the World Is the Desert?

Answer: Peru
How to get there

Searching "Nazca Lines country" or "giant desert line drawings Peru" quickly leads to Wikipedia/Britannica-style pages confirming the drawings (the Nazca Lines) are in southern Peru, in the Ica region near the city of Nazca.

Why this is the only answer

Only one country contains these particular drawings — there is no other reasonable country a correct search would surface.

Source / fact used

Confirmed via multiple sources including Wikipedia ("Nazca lines") and CuscoPeru.com, both describing the lines as located in the Ica region of southern Peru, near the city of Nazca.

Mission 01 · Easy · Part 2: Dutch Language

Where in the World Is the Desert? (Dutch check)

Answer: de woestijn
How to get there

"Woestijn" (desert) is a common-gender ("de-woord") Dutch noun, like "de stad" (the city) or "de rivier" (the river). It takes "de," not "het."

Why this is the only answer

Dutch nouns are fixed as either "de" or "het" words in the dictionary — "woestijn" is standardly a de-word, so "het woestijn" is simply incorrect Dutch.

Source / fact used

Standard Dutch grammar/dictionary knowledge (de/het noun classification for "woestijn").

Mission 02 · Easy · History

The Lady of the Lines

Answer: Maria Reiche
How to get there

Searching "Lady of the Lines" or "Nazca Lines scientist" leads directly to Maria Reiche, a German-born mathematician who first saw the lines in 1941 alongside American historian Paul Kosok, then dedicated the rest of her life (she died in 1998) to mapping, cleaning, and protecting them.

Why this is the only answer

"Lady of the Lines" is Maria Reiche's well-documented, widely used nickname — no other person is called this in connection with the Nazca Lines.

Source / fact used

Confirmed via Wikipedia ("Maria Reiche"), World History Encyclopedia ("The Nazca Lines: A Life's Work"), and a Smithsonian Magazine article describing her decades-long work and nickname.

Field Notebook fact (Mission 02) · used again in the Grand Finale

Delayed-recall detail: 18 shapes

Maria Reiche mapped the desert by hand and identified 18 different kinds of animals and birds among the drawings. This number is planted here (not inside the graded puzzle) and is required again, unprompted, in the Grand Finale (Mission 10) to compute 18 ÷ 3 = 6, which points to the 6th word of the finale's sentence: "wisdom."

Source / fact used

Confirmed via World History Encyclopedia ("The Nazca Lines: A Life's Work") and a Forbes article on the Google Doodle honoring Maria Reiche, both stating she identified 18 different animal/bird shapes.

Mission 03 · Medium · Mathematics (geometry/measurement)

The Ceremonial Plaza Puzzle

Answer: 800 (square meters)
How to get there

Area = ((top side + bottom side) ÷ 2) × height = ((30 + 50) ÷ 2) × 20 = (80 ÷ 2) × 20 = 40 × 20 = 800 m².

Why this is the only answer

The trapezoid's three given measurements (30 m, 50 m, 20 m) plug directly into the standard trapezoid area formula with no other reasonable reading — the diagram's pixel proportions were built to exactly match these numbers (1 m = 4 px throughout).

Source / fact used

Standard geometry formula (trapezoid area); the "large trapezoid plazas" flavor is a real, verified feature of Nazca geoglyphs (see Mission 05's Did-You-Know fact and World History Encyclopedia / National Geographic descriptions of trapezoids, triangles, and platforms used for gatherings).

Mission 04 · Medium · Part 2: Logical Reasoning (Foundational — pattern/sequence)

Eyes in the Sky (pattern)

Answer: 80
How to get there

5, 10, 20, 40 doubles each time (×2), so the next number is 40 × 2 = 80. (Equivalently, the gaps between terms are 5, 10, 20 — themselves doubling — so the next gap is 40, giving 40 + 40 = 80.)

Why this is the only answer

Both reasonable ways of reading the pattern (doubling the term, or doubling the gap) converge on the same answer, 80, so there is no ambiguity.

Source / fact used

Pure number-pattern logic puzzle; the framing ("lines crossing at a point") reflects a real, verified feature of the Nazca Lines — long straight lines really do converge at "ray centers" — though the specific counts (5/10/20/40) are invented for this puzzle, not a historical record.

Mission 05 · Medium · Navigation

Follow the Bearing

Answer: southeast
How to get there

Starting at North (0°) and moving clockwise in 45° steps: N=0°, NE=45°, E=90°, SE=135°. A bearing of 135° is exactly southeast.

Why this is the only answer

135° lands exactly on one of the 8 principal compass points (no rounding or interpretation needed), and the diagram's arrow is drawn at the mathematically correct angle for 135°.

Source / fact used

Standard compass bearing convention (clockwise from North). The flavor text about long, dead-straight Nazca lines running for kilometers on fixed bearings is a well-documented, real feature of the site.

Mission 07 · Hard · Critical Thinking & Media Literacy

Aliens, or Evidence?

Answer: YES
How to get there

A quick search on a reputable source confirms the Nazca Lines can indeed be seen from nearby hills and foothills, not only from the air. Archaeologist Toribio Mejía Xesspe first noticed the lines in 1927 while hiking a nearby hill, decades before airplanes were used to study them.

Why this is the only answer

This directly disproves Claim A's core premise ("can ONLY be seen from the air/space"), so Claim B (made by the Nazca people themselves, using simple tools, visible from hills) is the credible, evidence-based explanation. This mission deliberately avoids presenting the alien claim as possibly true — the point is that the specific, checkable fact (hills visibility) fails Claim A's premise.

Source / fact used

Confirmed via multiple sources describing archaeologist Xesspe's 1927 hilltop discovery and noting the lines remain visible from surrounding foothills and constructed viewing platforms, which refutes the "only visible from the sky" premise behind the ancient-astronaut claim. Also consistent with Maria Reiche's own well-documented rebuttal of the "landing strip" theory ("the ground is quite soft, I'm afraid the spacemen would have gotten stuck").

Big "Aha!" Moment · Multi-stage · Stage 2: Keyword-substitution cipher

The Professor's Coded Notebook (continued)

Stage 2 (final) answer: human hands
How to get there

Using Stage 1's number (8), count to the 8th word of "every sharp detective studies clues before revealing mystery": every(1) sharp(2) detective(3) studies(4) clues(5) before(6) revealing(7) mystery(8) → keyword = MYSTERY. Dropping the repeated letter (the second Y) gives M-Y-S-T-E-R, then the rest of the alphabet follows in order, skipping used letters: A,B,C,D,F,G,H,I,J,K,L,N,O,P,Q,U,V,W,X,Z. This produces the cipher row shown in the mission. Decoding BQHMI BMITO by looking up each letter in the cipher row and reading the plain-alphabet letter above it gives: B→H, Q→U, H→M, M→A, I→N = HUMAN; B→H, M→A, I→N, T→D, O→S = HANDS. Full message: HUMAN HANDS.

Why this is the only answer

Stage 2 cannot be solved without Stage 1's number 8 (it determines which word is the keyword, which determines the entire cipher alphabet). The substitution is one-to-one and was independently re-verified letter by letter in both directions.

Source / fact used

Keyword-substitution cipher construction is a standard, well-known cryptography technique; the "modern field-notebook code" framing avoids implying the real ancient Nazca people left any deciphered writing system (they did not — this is a fictional device used by the story's present-day archaeologist character, Dr. Quiroga).
