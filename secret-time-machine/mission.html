Internet Detective Academy · Case File

The Secret Time Machine

Agent briefing — read carefully before you begin
⏱ Estimated case time: 60-75 minutes

In the dusty back room of the Old Observatory Museum, behind a locked door nobody has opened in years, sits a brass-and-glass machine on wheels: the Chrono-Cycler. A yellowed tag says it was built by a forgotten inventor, Dr. Ada Vintner, who vanished the same week it stopped working — mid-jump, frozen between two moments in time.

The museum director just handed you the key. Every dial on the control panel is jammed, scrambled, or stuck on the wrong reading. To wake the machine up — and find out what happened to Dr. Vintner — you'll need to recalibrate it, dial by dial, using real facts, real maps, and real numbers. Get to work, Agent.

MISSION 01
EASY

The Flight Stabilizer Ring

The first thing you notice is a ring of five small brass gauges bolted around the main window. Dr. Vintner's notebook (open on the desk) says the ring only spins smoothly when every single gauge shows a creature that can actually fly.

Look closely at the five gauges below. One of them is jamming the ring. Which creature doesn't belong?

Every gauge on this ring is supposed to show a flying creature. Study each one carefully.
Hint 1

Four of these five creatures can truly fly through the air. Think about which one only jumps and climbs.

MISSION 02
EASY

The Spinning Compass Needle

Bolted to the side of the Chrono-Cycler is a brass compass dial. Dr. Vintner's notebook says it was pointing due North the moment the power surge hit.

During the surge, the needle spun clockwise three times in a row: first a half turn, then a quarter turn, then another half turn.

Which direction does the needle point now?

Hint 1

A half turn is 180°, and a quarter turn is 90°. Add up all three turns, then work out where that lands you starting from North and turning clockwise.

💡 Did you know? Before pendulum clocks existed, the best clocks in the world lost about 15 minutes a day. Christiaan Huygens' pendulum design (you'll meet him again later) shrank that error down to just 15 seconds a day!

MISSION 03
MEDIUM

The Control Panel's Missing Measurement

The Chrono-Cycler's main control panel is shaped like a wide base with a tall dial tower rising from its center — like a chimney on a house. A sticky note from Dr. Vintner says the panel's total surface area, in square centimeters, is the code that unlocks the tower's dial tray.

Use the measurements labeled on the diagram to find the total area of the whole panel (base + tower combined).

The tower's width is marked at the top, the base's full width along the bottom, the base's own height on the right, and the panel's total height on the left.
Hint 1

Split the whole shape into two ordinary rectangles: the wide base, and the narrow tower on top.

Hint 2

The tower's own height isn't labeled directly — but you can work it out: total height minus the base's height. Then find each rectangle's area and add them together.

MISSION 04
MEDIUM

The Brass Switch Labels

Four brass switches on the panel are labeled with old Dutch words for time: uur, jaar, moment, and tijd. Every Dutch noun needs the right little word in front of it — either "de" or "het".

Three of these four words use "het." One of them is different — it uses "de" instead. Which word is the odd one out?

Type just that one Dutch word (without its article).

Hint 1

Try each word with both articles: "de uur" or "het uur"? "de jaar" or "het jaar"? Search if you're not sure — Dutch grammar sites list this for every noun.

💡 Did you know? A day on Venus (about 243 Earth days, the time it takes to spin around once) is actually longer than its year (about 225 Earth days, the time it takes to circle the Sun)!

MISSION 05
HARD

The Power Dial's Rhythm

The Chrono-Cycler's power dial ticks up by a strange rhythm every time its gear turns. Dr. Vintner recorded the first five readings before the machine froze:

3, 6, 11, 18, 27, ?

What number comes next on the dial? (Figure out the rule the numbers follow, then use it to find the missing one.)

Hint 1

Look at how much the dial jumps up each time: from 3 to 6, from 6 to 11, from 11 to 18, from 18 to 27. Write those jumps down as their own list of numbers.

Hint 2

The jumps themselves go up by the same amount each time. Once you spot that pattern in the jumps, you can predict the next jump — and add it to 27.

MISSION 06
HARD

The Frozen World Clock

Set into the dashboard is a small "world clock" dial — two linked clock faces that are supposed to always agree on what time it really is in two cities at once. It's frozen showing 3:00 PM in Tokyo, Japan, on August 15, 2026.

The Chrono-Cycler is actually sitting in a lab in Amsterdam, the Netherlands. Search online to find the real time zones (UTC offsets) for Tokyo and for Amsterdam on that date — remember, Amsterdam runs on summer time in August, but Tokyo never changes its clocks at all.

What time is it in Amsterdam at that exact moment? Type your answer like this: 8:00 AM

Hint 1

Search "Tokyo time zone UTC offset" and "Amsterdam summer time UTC offset" separately — Japan uses one single offset all year, but Amsterdam's offset changes with the seasons.

Hint 2

Once you know both cities' distance from UTC, find the difference between them in hours. Tokyo is ahead of Amsterdam, so Amsterdam's clock reads earlier in the day.

💡 Did you know? Japan never changes its clocks for daylight saving — it stays on the exact same time zone all year round, while the Netherlands (and most of Europe) shifts its clocks forward and back twice a year.

MISSION 07
BIG AHA

The Era-Counter's Missing Number

Deep inside the Chrono-Cycler is its "era-counter" — a dial that's supposed to show a single number: the number of years between two completely different ways humans have kept time.

Search online to find two real facts:

1. What year did the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens build his first working pendulum clock? (Be careful — some pages mention a patent from a year later, but you want the year he actually built and tested it.)

2. What year was the world's first accurate atomic clock built?

Subtract the earlier year from the later year. That gap, in years, is the number the era-counter needs.

Hint 1

Try searching "Christiaan Huygens pendulum clock year built" and, separately, "first atomic clock year invented."

Hint 2

The pendulum clock came first, in the 1600s. The atomic clock came much later, in the 1950s. Subtract the smaller year from the bigger one.

GRAND FINALE
FINALE

Dr. Vintner's Last Nameplate

With every dial recalibrated, the Chrono-Cycler hums to life — and a small brass nameplate slides out from under the main window. It's engraved with a worn quote from a famous scientist who spent his whole life studying time, space, and the universe:

"I have no special talent. I am only passionately …"

Search online to find the rest of this real quote and who said it. The missing word describes a feeling — a hunger to explore, ask questions, and find things out.

Type the one-word noun for that feeling (not the "-ous" describing-word from the quote itself, but the "-ity" word for the feeling — like how "brave" becomes "bravery").

Hint 1

Search for the exact quote "I have no special talent" to find who said it and the missing word.

Hint 2

The describing-word in the quote ends in "-ous." The noun you need to type is the related word that ends in "-ity" instead.

Case closed?

The Chrono-Cycler's window fills with light, and a final message from Dr. Vintner scrolls across it: she didn't vanish by accident — she followed her own curiosity straight into history, chasing exactly the kind of questions you just answered. The machine was never really broken. It was just waiting for another curious mind to find it.
