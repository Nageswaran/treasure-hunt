Mission 02 · Easy

The Spinning Compass Needle

Answer: East
How to get there

Starting at North (0°), turning clockwise: a half turn (+180°) lands on South (180°). A quarter turn (+90°) lands on West (270°). Another half turn (+180°) lands on 450°, which wraps around past a full 360° circle to 90° — which is East.

Why this is the only answer

Compass rotation is fully deterministic: adding the three turns (180+90+180=450°) and reducing modulo 360° gives exactly one direction, East. There is no alternative reading of "clockwise" or "starting from North" that changes the result.

Source / fact used

Pure arithmetic/geometric reasoning on compass directions — no external source needed.

Mission 03 · Medium

The Control Panel's Missing Measurement

Answer: 380
How to get there

Split the "chimney on a house" shape into two rectangles. The base rectangle is 30 cm wide by 8 cm tall: 30 × 8 = 240 cm². The tower's height isn't labeled directly, but the total height (22 cm) minus the base's height (8 cm) gives the tower's height: 22 − 8 = 14 cm. The tower is 10 cm wide by 14 cm tall: 10 × 14 = 140 cm². Total area: 240 + 140 = 380 cm².

Why this is the only answer

The diagram's polygon coordinates were built directly from these same four labeled numbers (10, 8, 30, 22 cm at a scale of 6 px per cm, with the tower centered on the base), so the picture and the arithmetic always agree. Decomposing the shape any other way (e.g. splitting the tower and base along a different line) still yields the same two rectangle areas summing to 380 cm² — there is exactly one consistent total.

Self-drawn, to-scale composite-rectangle diagram (verified by re-deriving the area from the exact labeled dimensions: 240 + 140 = 380).

Mission 04 · Medium

The Brass Switch Labels

Answer: tijd
How to get there

"Het uur" (the hour), "het jaar" (the year), and "het moment" (the moment) are all "het-woorden." "De tijd" (the time) is the odd one out — it takes "de" instead of "het."

Why this is the only answer

Dutch noun gender is fixed per word (not a matter of opinion), and all four articles were checked against standard Dutch reference sources. Only "tijd" pairs with "de"; the other three all pair with "het," so "tijd" is the single defensible odd one out.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search against Dutch grammar references (ANW Algemeen Nederlands Woordenboek and standard Dutch de/het word lists): "het uur," "het jaar," "het moment," "de tijd."

Mission 05 · Hard

The Power Dial's Rhythm

Answer: 38
How to get there

The jumps between numbers are 3, 5, 7, 9 (6−3=3, 11−6=5, 18−11=7, 27−18=9) — each jump is exactly 2 more than the last. So the next jump is 9+2=11, and 27+11=38.

Why this is the only answer

The first differences (3, 5, 7, 9) form a perfectly constant-second-difference pattern, the classic signature of a quadratic sequence (this one equals n²+2 for n=1,2,3,4,5, confirmed by checking every given term). We stress-tested an alternative "multiply and adjust" reading (a(n+1)=2×a(n) minus a perfect square) and it produces the exact same values and the same next term, 38 — it's just a different way of describing the same sequence, not a competing answer. A ratio-based reading (each term roughly ×1.5 to ×2) does not land on a clean integer (27×1.5=40.5), so it isn't a defensible alternative. 38 is the only value consistent with the clearly visible, constant +2 growth in the jumps.

Source / fact used

Solved directly from the given sequence by hand (pure logical reasoning, no outside lookup required, as intended for this domain).

Mission 06 · Hard

The Frozen World Clock

Answer: 8:00 AM
How to get there

Japan Standard Time is UTC+9 year-round (Japan does not observe daylight saving time). On August 15, 2026, Amsterdam is on Central European Summer Time (CEST), UTC+2 (the Netherlands' clocks move forward on the last Sunday of March and back on the last Sunday of October — 2026's summer-time period runs March 29 to October 25, so August 15 falls inside it). The difference between the two offsets is 9−2 = 7 hours, with Tokyo ahead. 3:00 PM in Tokyo is 6:00 AM UTC (15:00−9), and 6:00 AM UTC + 2 hours = 8:00 AM in Amsterdam, same day.

Why this is the only answer

Both UTC offsets are real, fixed, and unambiguous for the stated calendar date (Japan never shifts; Amsterdam's CEST window for 2026 is fixed and confirmed), so the 7-hour gap and resulting 8:00 AM are the only correct conversion. The puzzle names a specific date precisely so the DST-dependent Amsterdam offset isn't ambiguous.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search: Japan Standard Time is UTC+9 with no DST (Wikipedia, "Japan Standard Time"); Netherlands 2026 DST dates are March 29 (start) and October 25 (end), CEST = UTC+2 (timeanddate.com, "Daylight Saving Time Changes 2026 in Amsterdam, Netherlands").

Mission 07 · Big Aha

The Era-Counter's Missing Number

Answer: 299
How to get there

Christiaan Huygens built and successfully tested his first working pendulum clock in 1656 (he patented the design the following year, 1657 — the puzzle steers the child toward the "built" year to avoid that mix-up). The world's first accurate atomic clock, a caesium atomic clock, was built in 1955 by Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the National Physical Laboratory in the UK. 1955 − 1656 = 299 years.

Why this is the only answer

Both years are well-documented and consistently reported across reputable sources when searched with the puzzle's exact wording ("built," not "patented"). The only realistic source of ambiguity — 1656 (built) versus 1657 (patented) — is explicitly flagged in the puzzle text so the child lands on 1656, keeping the subtraction unique at 299.

Source / fact used

Verified via web search: Christiaan Huygens completed his first pendulum clock in 1656 and patented it in 1657 (American Physical Society, "Christiaan Huygens Patents the First Pendulum Clock"; multiple encyclopedic sources agree on 1656 as the build year). First atomic clock: 1955, Louis Essen and J.V.L. Parry, National Physical Laboratory (Science Museum Group Collection, "Caesium Atomic Clock, 1955"; NPL "Louis Essen" history page).
